The first two books were written in partnership between a human writer and Grok Bot Lena Literary, an AI drafting assistant. The assistant wrote the prose; the human writer chose the story, set its rules, and sent the drafts back until they read right. The third, fourth and fifth books were written with a second AI assistant, Claude. What follows is a short record of that steering, in the order it happened.

The Premisechosen before a word of prose was written

The human writer began with a pairing: Sherlock Holmes and Phileas Fogg, two exacting Londoners whose lives overlap for some thirty years, Fogg ageing from his fifties into his seventies while Holmes rises from obscurity into his prime. The backdrop was to be the whole late Victorian age: industry, Empire, unrest, and the old Queen's long ending.

The assistant drafted three possible story shapes. The human writer layered on requirements one at a time, then picked The Wager Ledger: five self-contained meetings between 1881 and 1909, with Fogg's ledger of private wagers as the thread, and a dry, witty, clockwork tone.

The Writer’s Rulesset by the human writer, kept in every draft

  1. Hints, never names. Nods to Around the World in Eighty Days and to Holmes’s cases must stay buried in objects, habits, and asides, with no titles, characters, or events named outright.
  2. Companions in the margins. Watson and Passepartout appear only briefly, as warm, talkative foils to two cold principals, saving larger roles for later stories.
  3. A short cast list of ghosts. Inspector Fix as a bitter, failed would-be detective; an ageing Aouda as Fogg’s one crack; one meeting between the Baker Street boys and Passepartout.
  4. Felt, not stated. Both men treat life as a solvable equation, and the cost of that should land in a late scene without anyone saying it.
  5. Quality over speed. Polished prose, one segment at a time, with a check-in after each.

Book the First: The Snuffboxthree drafts to an unforced meeting

The first draft had Holmes cross a card room to Fogg, the two trading deductions, and the ledger noting Holmes as a man “to be watched.” The human writer judged it staged, and asked the assistant to say honestly whether it agreed. It did.

The meeting should feel as accidental as Holmes and Watson’s in the hospital laboratory: two men colliding through pure circumstance, neither caring about the other.

The second draft made the encounter accidental. The human writer then reshaped it once more: Holmes should notice an unnamed gentleman and count his steps through the fog before the reader learns who he is, and the thing that catches Holmes’s eye should be that man’s composure when his whist partner is exposed as a cheat. The third draft did exactly that, keeping Fogg nameless until the ledger’s flyleaf in the closing line. The human writer approved it.

Book the Second: The Cablewritten to the human writer’s brief

For 1887 the human writer specified the case (a forged telegram at the Reform Club that nearly ruins a member), the Golden Jubilee as atmosphere rather than exposition, the gas-jet clue, and the boys trading London shortcuts with Passepartout. The assistant built the plot inside those walls: a cable interruption recorded in Fogg’s ledger proves the “Cape” telegram was written in London.

One problem surfaced in the drafting. Holmes was to be newly famous, but the first account of his work appeared in print only that November, months after the June Jubilee. The assistant made his fame come from Scotland Yard and the newspapers, with the doctor’s manuscript still forthcoming, and offered the human writer the alternative of moving the story to December.

Book the Third: Fogg’s Wagera second assistant, and a gate of twenty lines

Before the third book, the first two were scored against a rubric of 28 dimensions, and the result was reduced to a gate of twenty lines that each new story must pass. The second assistant, Claude, proposed three briefs for 1894 and recommended one: an assurance office is to pay, at noon, on the death of Sherlock Holmes, while Fogg’s ledger holds a three-year wager that the man is not dead. The human writer accepted it and gave it its working title.

The human writer then added two elements for every book to come: threads that run quietly from story to story, and guests from other Victorian fiction. The assistant kept the guests to hints, with no names, under the first rule. A table of dates, weekdays and ages was drawn up before the draft, so that every figure in the story could be checked against it. The assistant wrote one full draft of nine scenes. The human writer read it and asked for no change.

The four pictures of the first two books were the reference for the faces. Ten images were made for four scenes; the human writer sent back a hand that gripped a lamp and a figure drawn too short, and in the end kept three plates and left the fourth scene without one.

Book the Fourth: The Hundred and Ninth Chequesix decisions, each with a recommendation

The brief for 1901 was a payment that stops: a cheque has come to a house in Amsterdam four times a year for twenty-seven years, and the hundred and ninth does not come. The human writer asked to be guided through each decision in turn. The assistant put six of them, each with its options and a recommendation: why the two had parted, the day of the first payment, the two days of the story, the lady’s home and her train, the clue, and her age. The human writer answered all six, and then changed the title from The Annuity to the one the book now bears.

The dates were checked before the draft. A script counted the 109 quarter-days from Christmas Day 1873 and gave the weekday of each, and the evening boat train was taken from a railway advertisement of 1901. The two elements of the third book were carried on: a guest of that book returns to give the clue, and a new guest travels on the lady’s train, both without names. The assistant wrote one full draft of ten scenes. The human writer approved it.

Two images were made, one for each of two scenes, with the earlier plates as the reference for the faces. A false signature in the corner of one was painted out. The human writer accepted both.

Book the Fifth: The First Pagein the manner of Conan Doyle

For the fifth book the human writer asked for the voice and tone of Arthur Conan Doyle. The assistant measured Doyle’s stories and the first four books on nine axes, and found the books short of Doyle in talk, in sentence length and in words of reasoning. The human writer kept the third person, as before, but in Doyle’s style.

The story is set in December 1909: an old man who once followed Mr. Fogg sends Holmes his file of thirty-seven years, a day before a Sunday newspaper goes to press. The assistant first wrote a creative draft, with the events, the clue and the dates; the human writer handed it to a fresh session of Claude, which wrote the book in nine scenes, about twice the length of the fourth.

Three pictures were made. The first versions had faults, among them a cardboard carton where a wooden box belonged and a hive fitting that looked modern. The human writer asked for the pictures to be fixed, and four edits followed.

Book the Sixth: The Broomwaya different kind of story

Before the sixth book the human writer asked for a review of the first five, so that later books would not repeat their plots. The assistant found one shape in most of them: a respectable London man misuses a paper, and Holmes exposes him in a room by setting two dated records side by side. The human writer asked for more variety in places, actions and plots, with the same characters, history and mood, and for every later book to keep Doyle’s voice in the third person.

The story is set in November 1896, between the third book and the fourth. A carrier has drowned on the Broomway, the tidal track across the Maplin Sands to Foulness Island, which is marked by brooms set in the sand. Most of the scenes are out of doors, and Mr. Fogg crosses the sands at night, in fog, by count and compass. A research assistant checked the Broomway, the island and the tides against period sources, and some names were changed because they had belonged to real people of the island. The assistant wrote the book itself, from its own plan, and the human writer accepted its recommendations.

Three pictures were made, one of them for the cover. The model wrote a caption into one picture and gave Mr. Fogg side-whiskers in another; the caption was cut away, and an edit of the head made him clean-shaven, as in the earlier plates.

Book the Seventh: Three Offencesa comic case, told gravely

For the seventh book the human writer chose the years 1897 to 1900, asked for some humour, and asked for a plan with strict rules that a fresh writer could follow. The assistant set the book on Mafeking Night, the eighteenth of May 1900, and brought in Holmes’s elder brother and his club, where no member may speak to another. A sack of army boots with brown paper in the soles is hidden in the club, and somebody means to burn it before a contract is signed on Monday.

One session of Claude wrote the plan and the rules; a later session wrote the prose in ten scenes, measured each scene against Doyle’s share of talk, and reported each place where it had departed from the plan. The human writer accepted the text.

Three pictures were made, one of them for the cover, and the faces were checked one by one against the plates of the earlier books. The model signed the cover with a false signature, which was painted out; the other small faults were accepted.

Book the Eighth: The Long Midnighta chase to Paris against two clocks

Before the eighth book the assistant listed what the seventh and sixth books shared, so that the eighth should not repeat it, and offered eleven new elements and three periods. The human writer accepted the recommendations: March 1911, after the last book in story time; a theft from the house in Savile Row; a journey to France; a clue that is felt in the hand; and Mr. Fogg’s own exactness turned against him. The lady has her first large part in the series, and the turn of the case is hers.

The night of the story is a real one. On the night of the tenth to the eleventh of March 1911 France put its clocks back nine minutes and twenty-one seconds, to the hour of Greenwich, and the assistant checked the law and the railway notice before it fixed a minute. One session of Claude wrote the outline, the minute table and the ten scenes without a separate draft, measured them against Doyle’s share of talk, and gave the text to a fresh reader, which found errors of arithmetic, of road and rail, and of the earlier books; they were corrected.

Three pictures were made, one of them for the cover, from crops of the faces in the earlier plates. Two false signatures were painted out, the hands of the clock in the cover were set to seven minutes past twelve as the text requires, and a second stone that the model had put in the lady’s other hand was removed.
