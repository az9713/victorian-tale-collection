The telegram came to Baker Street at ten minutes past four on the afternoon of Wednesday, the sixteenth of May, 1900, and Holmes, having read it twice, tossed it across the table to his friend.

MY CLUB DOES NOT BALANCE. COME AT A QUARTER TO FIVE AND NOT BEFORE. STRANGERS ROOM. MYCROFT.

"From your brother?" said the doctor.

"From my brother, who has sent for me once in five years, and who regards a telegram as a form of shouting." Holmes went to the window, where every second man below held an evening paper open. "You see what the town is reading. 'Still Waiting.' It will talk of nothing in the world but one small town in Africa until it has its news, and in the middle of it my brother leaves his line. His life is laid down like a railway, Doctor: his rooms, his office, his club, and his rooms again. When he sends for me, the train has left its metals and gone off across the fields to knock at a farmhouse door."

"He seems in no great hurry. 'Not before.'"

"On the contrary, he is in a very great hurry. He wrote that at his own desk, with the door shut, and in some disturbance of mind."

"My dear Holmes, how can you possibly know where he wrote it?"

"It is perfectly evident; it is printed upon the form. Handed in at Whitehall at two minutes past four. Mycroft leaves his office at half past four, and he is in his chair in Pall Mall at a quarter to five. A man who will not wait half an hour to say a thing, but sends a boy running with it, must have a thing upon his mind which he cannot carry for thirty minutes. And 'not before' is the most suggestive word in it. His chair is empty until a quarter to five, and he will not sit in the Stranger's Room to wait for me, because in the Stranger's Room he might be spoken to."

"Then it is a club matter?"

"It is the Diogenes Club, where no member may speak to another or take the least notice of him, and where three offences bring a man before the committee. My brother was one of its founders. I have always found it very soothing."

"But, Holmes," said the doctor, following him to the door, "what in the world can fail to balance in a club where nobody does anything?"

The Diogenes Club stood some little distance from the Carlton, and its hall was so quiet that the doctor heard his own boots upon the tiles as if they belonged to somebody else, and through a panel of glass he saw a great room in which some forty gentlemen sat, each in his own nook behind his own paper, while along the wall of the hall their hats hung upon a row of numbered pegs, like a second congregation which had come in out of the rain and been forbidden to speak. Beside the pegs a printed card in a black frame said: IN CASE OF FIRE MEMBERS ARE REQUESTED TO RING TWICE.

A grey-whiskered hall porter, whom Holmes called Battersby, showed them into the small room which looks out upon Pall Mall, and spoke as soon as the door was shut.

"You will forgive my not knowing you in the hall, sir. I know no gentleman in the hall. A gentleman who walks past me as though I were not there is a member, sir. It is the only test the rules leave me."

At a quarter to five exactly Mycroft Holmes came in. He was a much larger and heavier man than his brother, with a massive face which had kept the sharpness of the family, and eyes of a peculiarly light and watery grey. He gave the doctor a broad hand like the flipper of a seal, lowered himself into the largest chair, and took snuff from a tortoise-shell box, brushing the grains from his waistcoat with a large red silk handkerchief.

"Sherlock," said he, "I shall give you the facts once, in their order, for I do not like to repeat a remark. Every evening at seven three numbers are brought to my chair. Battersby sends me a card with the number of hats upon the pegs, for the porter may count hats, you understand, but he may not notice heads. The page who sees to the lights sends me a second card with the number of gentlemen in the rooms upstairs, which he counts because the committee pays for the electric light and dislikes to light an empty room. The third is my own: the great room, which I count from my chair. Strangers are not counted, and their hats are kept in this room. For nineteen evenings the gentlemen have been one more than the hats. Forty-one hats, Sherlock, and forty-two men. A man who comes into this house and does not hang up his hat must have something under it. He never takes the same chair twice. On the eleventh evening a member passed behind my chair whose boots squeaked. No member of this club has squeaked in twenty years, and I should certainly have heard him. On the thirteenth the housekeeper entered in her book of complaints that the girl had found candle grease upon the back stair. This house has been lit by electricity since 1893, and nobody in it has carried a candle since, unless he had a reason. On the sixteenth the page reported that the back stair smelt of a lamp, and nobody in this house has trimmed a lamp for seven years. I have observed all this without once leaving my chair, and I do not propose to leave it now. Whatever is to be followed up two pairs of stairs is not my business. I should not do it well, and I should not do it at all."

"Then lay the facts before the committee," said Holmes.

"It is impossible. To bring him before the committee I must say that I have observed him for nineteen evenings, which is nineteen offences, and I have only three to spend. Moreover, the committee can deliberate only in this room, and in this room it would be obliged to hear me. No, Sherlock. A member who reports another member admits that he took notice of him, and that is the offence."

"And what does he want, this gentleman who will not part with his hat?"

"He wants a sack," said Mycroft Holmes, folding away his handkerchief. "It lies in a trunk in the box-room at the top of this house, under my seal, because I trust no room in the War Office, and this is the one house in London where nobody will take notice of a sack. It holds four-and-twenty pairs of boots, taken from the feet of Yeomanry troopers who have been sent home invalided from the Cape. Each pair has a label in the hand of a hospital sergeant: the trooper's name, his corps, and the miles he marched. Nine pairs went through water, and they are boots no longer. Fifteen came from men who were sent home with fever before the wet, and their soles are whole. I need both kinds. The nine show what happens, and the fifteen show that it was not the men who did it. Every sole of the four-and-twenty has brown paper in it. At noon on Monday the Director of Army Contracts signs for sixty thousand pairs with the firm that sold these, unless the sack is upon his table first. The troopers are scattered over half England. There is no second set."

"And the firm?"

"Tolladay and Company, of Kettering. Sir Enoch Tolladay, who was knighted last year, and who has a friend among the clerks of the Contracts branch, which is the reason that the War Office is out of the question."

"A remarkable statement, Mycroft. Has anybody seen this man's face?"

"Nobody in this house is permitted to see his face, Sherlock, and I am sure that nobody has. But one member has two offences against him already, and a man in that position looks about him."

"Then I should like to meet that member very much," said Holmes. "Tell me, Mycroft: in a house where no man may look at another, who has looked at this one?"

The member with two offences was shown into the Stranger's Room at twenty minutes to six, a pleasant, round-faced, untidy gentleman of about forty, with a humorous mouth which he had plainly been at pains to keep shut, and he shook hands with both of them before he could be prevented.

"You don't know what this is," said he. "To speak! I joined this club because I was told it was a club for men who did nothing, and I felt qualified. Nobody mentioned that one was to do it without speaking. I have two offences against me: I said 'Bless you' to a gentleman who sneezed, and I laughed aloud at a leading article, which had not happened in the club before, to that paper. One more, and I go. Does evidence count, Mr. Holmes?"

"In this room you may say what you please, and the committee cannot hear it," said Holmes. "Pray tell us about the gentleman who keeps his hat."

"Well, I noticed him, I'm afraid. I have a medical turn of mind. I once read through a medical book in the British Museum and found that I had every disease in it except housemaid's knee, and I have never quite got over the slight, so I look at anybody who seems ill, in case he has got it, and he seemed ill. He was a small, neat man of five-and-thirty or so, with a frightened sort of face, and he carried his hat upon his knees as if there were eggs in it. Not a hat that a man hangs up, but a hat that a man guards. Every evening, and in a different chair, and never once did he read the paper that he held. And he smelt of a lamp: not a lamp that has been lit, but a lamp that has been spilt. I once carried two cheeses for a friend from Liverpool to London in a railway carriage, and I had the carriage to myself from Crewe, and at Euston the porters—"

"The smell of lamp oil," said Holmes gently. "Upon his clothes, or upon his hat?"

"Upon the hat, now you mention it. He went upstairs by the back way, too, and when he came down the hat was lighter. And his boots squeaked, like a new pair in church: a little hesitating squeak at every step, as if the boots had a doubt about something and didn't like to mention it. My uncle had a pair like that. He put them on to hang a picture once, and he called the whole household up to hold the ladder, and then—"

"Your uncle shall have our full attention another day," said Holmes. "The boots, if you please."

"I beg your pardon. On the Monday he put his feet up on the fender of the reading-room, which is not an offence, though I think it ought to be, and I saw the backs of his heels. There was a little brass bulldog on the tab of each boot, the tab you pull them on by: a bulldog sitting up, with its chest out. It seemed to me a very cheerful thing to have upon a boot, and he was not a cheerful man. That is all, Mr. Holmes."

Since speech was as much forbidden upon the back stair as in the great room, Holmes and the doctor went up it in silence, Holmes creeping from step to step with his lens, and in the box-room at the top, among the trunks of members who had died or gone abroad, he lay down at full length behind the largest of them and rose again with an inch of candle, burnt down and pinched out, in the palm of his hand. The lock of Mycroft Holmes's trunk had not been touched, and the seal upon the sack within it was whole. At Holmes's request the sack went down that night to the porter's own room, and the trunk was locked again upon nothing.

"The inference is perfectly clear, Doctor. A drop of grease upon every third stair, and the drops are round," said Holmes in the Stranger's Room. "That is evidently a man carrying a lighted candle up a stair, slowly. Behind the trunks, where no light could be seen from the door, there is a ring of grease upon the boards, and this stood in it. The box-room has a window, and therefore he did not need a light to see by. He let it burn, and came back, and pinched it out. A man who tries a candle where nobody will see it must be finding out how long he may be away. He has measured his time, Doctor. Who in London can measure it for me as exactly as he has?"

The great hospital at Netley lies for a quarter of a mile along Southampton Water, and the doctor, who had been taught his trade in it two-and-twenty years before, had not expected to see its long corridors again, but at eleven o'clock on the Thursday morning he walked down one of them beside a sergeant of the Medical Corps, who carried a register of boots under his arm as other men carry a prayer-book.

"Every pair through my hands, sir," said the sergeant. "Name, corps, and the miles marched, as near as the man could tell. The gentleman in Whitehall asked for the Yeomanry only. There's one of them in the end bed now, if you'd care to see what a pair of those boots can do to a man."

The trooper in the end bed was a big, fair, quiet young fellow of three-and-twenty, a farmer's son, with both his feet in bandages upon a cradle. He let the doctor undo them, and spoke slowly, looking at the ceiling.

"They were good boots on the ship, sir, and good on the veldt while it was dry. You could have danced in them. The colonel bought them for the whole squadron, and we were proud of them, for the Line had nothing so smart. Then we came to a river. It wasn't much of a river. At home you'd have called it a brook. But we were all day crossing it with the waggons, and by the morning my boots had gone like a wet newspaper. The soles came away from the uppers, and there was brown paper inside, sir, stuck to my socks: brown paper, like you'd wrap a pound of tea in. I did the last nine miles into camp on the uppers, and then on the socks, and then on what was under them. I'm not complaining of the colonel. He meant well by us. He paid for them himself."

The doctor examined the feet for some time, turning each gently to the window.

"These are clearly not marching sores, Sergeant," said he at last. "A man who marches too far blisters at the heel and at the ball of the foot, and the skin between is sound. These go across the whole of the sole, and they are full of grit. The explanation is dreadful, and simple. He did not march too far. He marched upon nothing."

"That's what I've written against every one of the nine, sir," said the sergeant, "though not in those words." He turned up the trooper's boot, which hung by its laces from the rail of the bed, and held it out. Upon the tab at the back of the heel, where a man takes hold to pull it on, there was a small brass bulldog, sitting up.

"We thought it handsome," said the trooper.

He lay looking at the ceiling for a while, and then he turned his head.

"There's the rest of my squadron out there in them yet, sir, and more rivers ahead of them. Can you get me sent back before they come to the next one?"

The card room of the Reform Club is not a place in which much is said, and on the afternoon of Thursday the greater part of what was said in it was said by Sir Enoch Tolladay, a big, high-coloured man of six-and-fifty with the hero of Mafeking upon a button in his lapel and a brass bulldog upon his watch-chain, who was partnering Mr. Fogg.

"Hearts, partner, hearts! You had them, I knew you had them," said he. "Never says a word, gentlemen, and never needs to. That's the man I want opposite me. You'll be at this table to-morrow night, Mr. Fogg? From eight o'clock till eleven? I like a steady man opposite me on a Friday. No answer? Silence gives consent, sir, and I'll take it for yes." He turned to the doctor by the fire. "You're looking at my dog, sir. Bulldog Brand: I shoe the Yeomanry of five counties. There's not a squadron gone out from the Midlands since Christmas that isn't marching in Tolladay's, and I'd march in them myself if they'd take a man of my weight. I gave a hundred guineas to the Reservists' fund this morning, and I don't care who knows it. Why should I? A man who has done well out of his country ought to put something back, and say so, so that the next man feels the draught. There, partner: game and rubber. Back directly, gentlemen. There's a fellow in the smoking-room who wants to sell me a hunter, and I mean to let him try."

The doctor, who had seen that brass dog once already that day, upon a boot at Netley, looked quickly at his friend, but Holmes was studying the cards, and he took the empty chair.

"Mr. Fogg, a man has come into my brother's club nineteen evenings with his hat upon his knees. He has timed a candle there; here is the end of it. In that house lies a sack of boots which must be in Whitehall at noon on Monday, and somebody means that it shall not. I want this candle's twin timed exactly, and to-morrow I want a man in that house who will see every movement and judge none."

"I was at Netley this morning," said the doctor. "There is a trooper there of three-and-twenty who walked the last nine miles of a march upon the uppers of a pair of these boots, and then upon his feet. There was brown paper in the soles."

"How many paces?" said Mr. Fogg, with his watch in his hand.

"Paces, Mr. Fogg? It is a curious question. I don't quite follow you."

"He means the march, Doctor," said Holmes. "What does a column do in a day, upon its own feet, when the horses are done?"

"Twenty miles, on a good day, and more when the enemy is in front."

"Forty-two thousand two hundred and forty," said Mr. Fogg.

Holmes laid beside the end a new candle of the same make, and Mr. Fogg marked it with his thumbnail at the height of the old, and wrote an order for Savile Row.

To be lit at 6h 0m this evening, upon a plate, in still air. Note the minute at which the flame reaches the mark, and sign it.—P. F.

"The Diogenes Club," said Mr. Fogg, "at eight minutes to five."

"My brother will be at the window," said Holmes. "He will not come to the door."

Sir Enoch came back for another rubber, and talked through the whole of it.

"Bought him, gentlemen, and paid too much, and I don't regret a penny of it. Your lead, Mr. Fogg. A grand night it'll be when the news comes, and come it will, mark my words, before the week's out. I've a flag ready in Kettering as big as this room, and a barrel for the men. They're good lads at Tolladay's. My foreman's as sober a little chap as ever cut a sole, and he's never asked me for a holiday in ten years. Trumps, partner? Thank you. Never a word, and never a mistake: I could play with this man till I died." He paused in the doorway. "It's a great comfort, gentlemen, a partner who never opens his mouth. Friday, then, Mr. Fogg? Eight till eleven?"

Holmes sent the doctor to Savile Row that evening as an umpire, on the ground that a timing which nobody had watched was a timing which nobody would believe, and at six o'clock precisely the doctor sat down in the pantry upon a kitchen chair and watched the steward light a candle upon a plate. The little man had shut the window, stopped the crack under the door with a rolled cloth, and set beside the plate the enormous silver watch of his great-grandfather, and from that moment he did not take his eyes from the flame.

"Three hours, perhaps four, monsieur," said he. "It is long. You will permit that I talk? In the house of Monsieur one does not talk, and I have saved a great deal."

"Talk by all means," said the doctor. "I should be glad of it."

"Then I tell you my trades, for I have had a great many. I was a singer in the streets when I was a boy, at Lyon and at Paris, for sous. Then I was with a circus. I was a rider, and I vaulted over the horses like Léotard, and I danced upon the rope like Blondin, with a pole, and a little umbrella for the ladies. Then I was a professor of gymnastics, because a man who has danced upon a rope can teach anybody to stand upon a floor. And then, monsieur, I was four years with the firemen of Paris, and I was a sergeant. We went to every fire in our quarter, by day and by night, with a pump upon wheels and a brass helmet that one polished on Sundays. That is the trade I have not forgotten. A fireman knows three things that other men do not know. He knows that the good air is upon the floor. When the smoke comes, a man goes à plat ventre, upon his stomach, like a little child, and he lives. He knows that a fire is honest: it goes up. It goes always up, and it never comes down a stair unless you leave it nothing else to do. And he knows that a door is a decision. You open it, and you give the fire a breath, and you shut it, and you give it a room. Since 1872, monsieur, I am a man extremely to be trusted with a flame."

"It is a remarkable history," said the doctor slowly, "and I think that a house in which a man tries candles is a house which wants a fireman."

"I have thought it also, monsieur, since five o'clock. This candle is honest. It has no draught to make it hurry. But a candle is like a horse, monsieur. It goes as it is ridden."

"You have carried men out of fires?"

"Eleven, monsieur, and one lady, and two dogs. A man you carry upon the shoulder, so, with his arm over your neck and your hand upon his knee, and you go down the ladder backwards, and you do not look at the street."

At eight minutes to ten the flame drew down to the mark which Mr. Fogg's thumbnail had made, and touched it, and the steward pinched it out, looked at his watch, wrote the minute beneath the order in a round, careful hand, and signed his name to it.

"Nine hours fifty-two minutes, monsieur. Three hours and fifty-two from the match." He folded the paper and gave it to the doctor, and at the door he coughed. "Would Monsieur Holmes wish me to be in Pall Mall to-morrow night, monsieur, in case the candle has a brother?"

At a quarter to five on the Friday the two brothers sat in the bow-window of the Stranger's Room, with the doctor behind them, looking out into Pall Mall.

"Three hours and fifty-two minutes, by the steward's watch," said Holmes. "I shall be in the box-room from five o'clock, Mycroft, behind the trunks, with the empty one under my eye. If he comes to it, I have him. Mr. Fogg will be in the upper library, where the back stair comes out, and the steward will be here at nine, in case the candle has a brother."

"At seven I shall have the count," said Mycroft Holmes. "If he is in the house, it will not balance, and I shall know it, and you will not, Sherlock, until twenty minutes to eight, when I shall send you a card. I do not send cards before my hour. I shall be in my chair, where I always am, and where he will expect me to be."

"And if the count balances?" said the doctor.

"Then it is clear that the man has not come yet, and he will come late, and for the last time. There, Mycroft, by the lamp-post."

"He carries his watch in his left hand," said Mycroft Holmes, "and he does not look at it."

"It is obvious that he has no need to look at it. He must know already what it will tell him, and he carries it only to be told so. A man who consults his watch is in doubt of the time, and this man is never in doubt of it."

"His heels are worn so evenly that he evidently shares out his paces between his two feet as a banker shares out a sum."

"The hands of a whist-player: he keeps the right one free, and still."

"And he does not hurry for the kerb, Sherlock. I rather think that the kerb waits for him. I had five pounds upon him in 'seventy-two. I won. I never collected. The man lived in Kensington."

A tall white-haired gentleman came up the steps, walked past the porter as though he were not there, and opened the door of the Stranger's Room himself.

"Four fifty-two," said Mr. Fogg. "Your clock is forty seconds slow."

"Then I have left this house forty seconds late upon every evening since that clock was last set, which was at Lady Day," said Mycroft Holmes, and something like distress came into his heavy face. "It is the kind of thing a man would wish to be told. You will sit in the upper library, Mr. Fogg, until twenty minutes to eight. You will time every movement in that room, and you will judge none of them."

"Every movement," said Mr. Fogg, "and no judgment."

"Your hat and yourself will cancel each other in my count. I shall allow for you." Mycroft Holmes rang. "The gentleman's hat, Battersby."

Battersby, who knew his rules, had taken the visitor at once for a member of thirty years' standing, and hung the hat upon a peg accordingly. At five Holmes went up by the back stair to the box-room. At five minutes past, Mr. Fogg took a chair in the upper library, and Mycroft Holmes withdrew to his own chair in the great room.

At twenty minutes to eight the page carried a card up the back stair to the box-room.

43 hats. 43 men. He has not come. I dislike it extremely. M.

Holmes put it in his pocket and settled again behind the trunks. A minute later Battersby put his head in at the door of the Stranger's Room.

"I beg your pardon, sir. It is a minute past the time by our clock, which they tell me is slow, and Mr. Mycroft has not gone. His hat is on the peg, sir, and nobody has asked for it. I have had the door of this house for one-and-thirty years, and I have held it for him at twenty minutes to eight on every evening of them that he was in London. I could set the clock by him, sir, and now it seems I ought to have. It has been a very peculiar evening, sir. Is Mr. Mycroft unwell, sir?"

At ten past nine Holmes fetched Mr. Fogg from the upper library, and at a quarter past, in the Stranger's Room, where the steward had waited since five past, Mr. Fogg laid a sheet of paper upon the table.

5h 5m: seated; one gentleman asleep in the chair beside the cupboard.

5h 12m: a page, with a sack, to the cupboard; 2m.

5h 31m: a person, boots that squeak, pace of 29 inches, the left late; to the cupboard, 4m; out by the back stair.

7h 0m: a page, counting.

8h 46m: the gentleman beside the cupboard turns over.

9h 10m: relieved.

Holmes read it, and looked across at his brother.

"I moved it," said Mycroft Holmes. "At ten minutes past five, from Battersby's room to the cupboard beside Mr. Fogg, who is the best guard in London. To tell you I should have had to walk to this room. And at seven the count was right, and we both came to the same conclusion: that he had not come yet."

"You sent me a card at twenty to eight."

"With the count. A count is a fact, and I send facts. Where I keep a sack is a decision."

"Neither of us went up to ask the gentleman who was sitting beside the cupboard."

"I never go up, Sherlock. You know it."

From twenty-five minutes to six until a quarter past nine the one fact which mattered had lain upon a sheet of paper in the upper library, between a man who would not volunteer it and a man who would not walk to a room to ask for it, with the detective one floor above it and his brother one floor below: three hours and forty minutes.

"Thirty-one minutes past five, and three hours fifty-two," said Mr. Fogg. "Twenty-three minutes past nine. I can give you eight minutes."

"No, monsieur," said the steward.

"No," said Mr. Fogg, after a moment.

"It is evident," said the doctor. "The pantry was shut tight. But a cupboard has a crack under its door, and a back stair behind it, and where there is a draught a candle—"

It was nineteen minutes past nine, and smoke had come curling under the door. When Holmes flung it open a soft haze was pouring down the great staircase into the reading-room, where forty gentlemen looked at it with the deepest disapproval, and returned to them. Then the member with two offences, the only member who had ever read the card beside the pegs, rang twice, as it directed, and when nothing happened he rose and said, in a clear and carrying voice, the one word: "Fire!"

It woke nobody in the upper library, where the only sleeper was deaf, but it brought the steward into the hall at a run.

"Au feu! Messieurs, the carpet! The carpet of the great room, to the street, under the second window: twelve men to hold it. You, sir, and you, and you. Battersby, the buckets from the cistern, and a chain up the back stair, one man to a stair. No, monsieur, do not open that door. It is a decision. Doctor, your handkerchief, wet, upon your mouth. Monsieur, the back stair, if you please. Mr. Holmes, with me. À plat ventre, Mr. Holmes. The good air is upon the floor."

He was obeyed in silence, in the one house in London where nobody had ever given an order aloud. They went up upon their elbows and knees to the upper library, where the cupboard was one sheet of flame and the heat was terrible, and six feet from it an old gentleman in a velvet cap slept with his mouth open.

"The sack, Mr. Holmes! Drag it, do not lift it, and keep your face low. Doctor, the old one; we cannot take him by either stair, for the fire is between his chair and both doors. To the window, all of us. Wake him if you can, and if you cannot, carry him."

"He is deaf, steward," said the doctor, "and very heavy."

"Then he will not hear the crowd, monsieur, and the carpet will hold him."

"The sack is out, steward. The canvas is alight, but the boots are not."

The steward found the lower sash painted fast, threw up the upper one, and stepped out over it upon the narrow ledge above Pall Mall. He stood there in the smoke, quite upright, and drove the lower sash free with three blows of his heel, with a neatness that the doctor had not expected.

"In my youth I was on the rope, monsieur," said he, stepping back. "It is the same thing with more smoke. Messieurs below, hold it tight! He comes."

The street had filled from wall to wall, and it was singing, for the news had come. Below, twelve members of the Diogenes Club held the carpet taut at twelve points round its edge, and Mycroft Holmes stood at one of them as other men set a post. The old gentleman woke as they lifted him over the sill, said "Eh?" in a loud voice, and went down into the carpet with his cap in his hand, and the crowd cheered him for part of the rejoicing. The sack went after him.

"Now the buckets," said the steward. "Not so fast, messieurs. Slower, and all together. Take the time from Monsieur. The water to the foot of the fire, not to the top. The top is only smoke. Again."

The chain ran from the cistern up the back stair: forty of the most unsociable men in London, not one of whom took the least notice of the man who handed him his bucket, and it kept its time from Mr. Fogg, who stood half-way up it like the pendulum of a clock. At the head of the stair the steward stood black to the eyes, counting the buckets as they came.

"Soixante et onze. Soixante-douze. And the engine, messieurs? Where is the engine?"

The engine came at last, one-and-thirty minutes upon the last quarter of a mile, and by ten past ten the fire was out. A few doors off the street was singing to a board upon the War Office which had no news on it, and upon the club steps General Cobbold, in a blanket and his socks, addressed the fire officer.

"I have been taken notice of, sir, in my own club, by a Frenchman. I have been carried about like a parcel and thrown out of a window before half London, and cheered. I shall write to the committee in the morning."

"I like work," said the member with two offences, who had been in the chain, and had lost his collar. "It fascinates me. I could stand and look at it for hours, and to-night I have."

Mr. Fogg stood upon the top step with his watch in his hand, and for two minutes he watched ten thousand people walking every way at once beneath him, and then he pointed with the watch.

"That is his walk."

"You know him?" said the doctor.

"I have never seen him."

Holmes and the doctor went down into the crowd and came back with a small, neat, frightened man between them, who held his hat in both hands. His boots squeaked upon the wet step, and upon the tab of each heel sat a brass bulldog.

"I'm foreman at Tolladay's, sir. Pargeter. Thirty shillings a week, and the boots thrown in. I never wanted to do it. I was told it was an old house, and that it would be called an accident, and that there'd be nobody in the room but an old gentleman asleep, who'd wake. Sir Enoch said a fire in an old house was nobody's fault. I was to be home in Kettering by the last train. I'd have been home, too, only I wanted to see the old gentleman got out."

Under a lamp at the corner, a small elderly man with restless eyebrows was writing in a notebook while all London sang, and he looked up at the steps, and wrote again.

Sir Enoch Tolladay came shouldering through the crowd from the Reform Club a few minutes later.

"What's this, what's this? A fire, on such a night? Nobody hurt, I hope. My dear sir, your men have done nobly, nobly, with every soul in London out of his wits and singing, and I'll give fifty pounds to them this minute if you'll tell me where to send it. Bless my soul, Mr. Fogg! I looked for you at the table at eight o'clock, sir, and there was nobody opposite me but the dummy. You'll forgive an old partner for saying he missed you. It's a grand night, sir, a grand night, and you've missed the best of it." Then he saw the man beside Holmes, and stopped. "Pargeter? What the devil are you doing here?"

"He has been setting fire to the Diogenes Club, Sir Enoch, which has never been done before," said Holmes. "For once, Doctor, the fraud is not upon paper. The paper is in the fraud. On nineteen evenings there was one man too many in this house, and one hat too few, and a man who will not part with his hat has something in it. Here is the hat. The lining is stiff with lamp oil, and there is a shaving still caught in the band. He evidently carried his fire into this house a little at a time, upon his knees. Upon the back stair there was candle grease, in a house that has been lit by electricity for seven years, and in the box-room an inch of candle in a ring of grease. He must have timed a candle. Mr. Fogg's steward timed its twin last night, and it burned three hours and fifty-two minutes. That is the singular feature of the case: he knew the house, and not the room. A clerk of the Contracts branch must have told him that Mr. Holmes of the Audit had taken a sack to his club, and it cost him a fortnight of evenings to find the box-room. This afternoon the sack was carried across the hall by a page, and he followed it up the stair to the cupboard.

"A member of this club heard his boots squeak, which a sole of honest leather does not do. Layers of paper rub upon one another and squeak, and when they are wet, they open. Mr. Fogg wrote down that squeak at thirty-one minutes past five this afternoon, beside the cupboard which took fire. He has never seen this man's face, and he knew his walk just now among ten thousand. Then there is the bulldog. It was upon a trooper's boots at Netley yesterday, whose feet my friend examined. It is upon this man's boots to-night, and it hangs upon your watch-chain, Sir Enoch, where you showed it to us yesterday with a good deal of pride. Lastly, you asked Mr. Fogg two times yesterday to sit opposite you to-night from eight o'clock until eleven. Three hours and fifty-two minutes from thirty-one minutes past five is twenty-three minutes past nine. You asked for the most exact witness in London for the very hours in which this house was to burn. The conclusion is obvious. A man does not need such a witness unless he expects to need an alibi."

"He asked twice," said Mr. Fogg.

"Stuff!" cried General Cobbold, for Holmes had pitched his voice for him. "I bought those boots myself, sir, for my own regiment of the ——shire Yeomanry, at a shilling under Northampton, and they are as good boots as ever went on a horseman's foot. I'll not have it said that I put my lads into paper."

Holmes broke the seal of the scorched sack, and took out a pair by its label.

"This pair belonged to a trooper of your own corps, General. The label says that he went down with fever at the Cape before he had marched a mile, and he never wore them in the wet. They are whole. You have lost your slippers in the fire. You bought these, General, and you will find them comfortable for at least thirty yards."

The old soldier glared at him, then pulled the boots on and laced them with his own hands, rose, and set off through the water from the hoses towards the War Office, very upright, with the blanket hanging from his shoulders and the light of the street-lamps on his white head, while the crowd parted for him without knowing why. Mr. Fogg counted aloud upon the top step. At thirty-one the boots began to squeak, and the General turned at the board and came back. At eighty-four the left sole came away from the upper with a soft, sucking sound, and the old man stood still in the gutter, and looked down for a long time at his foot, and at the grey pulp of paper coming out of it.

"Eighty-four," said Mr. Fogg. "They are asked for forty-two thousand a day."

Sir Enoch's colour had gone.

"A bad pair. Any maker has a bad pair."

"There are fourteen more whole pairs in that sack, Sir Enoch, all of the same order," said Holmes. "Choose any one of them you please, and walk in it the hundred yards to the Reform Club, and I will withdraw every word."

Sir Enoch looked at the sack, and at the General's foot, and he did not put one on. Mycroft Holmes ended it from the top step.

"Your firm will be struck off the list of contractors on Monday morning, Sir Enoch. Every pair that you have sold to the Yeomanry will be made good at your cost by Northampton makers, pair for pair. Your clubs will hear of it without my saying anything, which is how this club hears everything. I do not think that we need detain you."

Pargeter was given in charge to a constable, and Sir Enoch went away into a crowd which took no notice of him whatever. Mycroft Holmes looked out over a street which had not been so loud since he was born.

"It is eleven o'clock," said he. "Will nobody in this house take notice of the fact that it is three hours and twenty minutes past my hour?"

The Stranger's Room smelt of wet carpet, and Holmes had gone with the constable and the foreman to the police office. Mr. Fogg sat at the table in a coat borrowed from the porter. He took the green book from his breast pocket, wrote in it, and turned it so that the doctor could read. Mycroft Holmes read it also, upside down, from his chair, without leave.

17 May. That a candle of this make, in still air, burns to the mark in 3h 52m.

Won. 3h 52m.

18 May. That I shall be relieved at 7h 40m.

Lost. 9h 10m. Cause: the brother of Venn's guest.

That the cupboard takes fire at 9h 23m.

Lost. 9h 19m. Cause: a draught.

"I have been called a number of things in two departments," said Mycroft Holmes, "but never yet a cause. It is just. I should have walked to this room." He considered the last line for some time. "You do not allow yourself the draught, sir."

"It was there to be reckoned."

"I thought as much," said Mycroft Holmes, after a pause. "I shall not ask where a man learns to disallow a draught. I should be obliged to verify it, and I never verify."

Mr. Fogg closed the book, and the doctor, who had heard every word, made nothing whatever of it. Mycroft Holmes took snuff, and for one second Mr. Fogg's eye rested upon the tortoise-shell lid of the box.

The committee sat at once, in that room, since it could sit in no other: Mycroft Holmes and two elderly gentlemen in damp coats.

"The word spoken at nineteen minutes past nine," said the first of them. "Our member's third offence. It is very painful."

"To whom was it addressed?" said Mycroft Holmes.

"To nobody, I suppose. He said it to the room."

"Then it was evidently a soliloquy, and the rule does not reach it. The rule forbids a member to speak to another member. It says nothing of a member who speaks to himself."

"And the Frenchman? He gave orders to the whole house. I counted a hundred and fourteen of them in one-and-thirty minutes."

"He is not a member. We cannot expel him. We can only thank him, and the secretary shall write to him in the morning."

"There remains General Cobbold, who complains that he was taken notice of."

"He was," said Mycroft Holmes. "We must uphold it, with regret. And then there is the clock."

The minute was written out by the secretary and passed across the table.

Resolved: (1) That the word spoken in the reading-room at 9.19 p.m. was addressed to no member, and was therefore a soliloquy, which the rule does not reach. (2) That a person not a member committed one hundred and fourteen offences in thirty-one minutes; that as he is not a member he cannot be expelled; and that the thanks of the committee be conveyed to him. (3) That the complaint of General Cobbold, that he was taken notice of, be upheld, with regret. (4) That the clock in the hall be put forward forty seconds.

"I should be glad to propose you for this club, Mr. Fogg," said Mycroft Holmes. "You are the only man in London who is already qualified."

"I have a club," said Mr. Fogg, and rose. "They do not play whist."

In the hall Battersby stood beside the pegs and counted the hats as the members took them down and went out into the noise, one by one, without a word to one another, and the number was right. Mycroft Holmes took his own hat from the porter's hand, and looked at the clock, which had not yet been put forward.

"You may set it in the morning, Battersby," said he. "I have been late enough for one evening."
