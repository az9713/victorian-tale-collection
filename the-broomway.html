"A man has been drowned upon a road, Doctor," said Holmes, tossing a letter across the breakfast-table, "and his daughter writes to ask whether a road can do such a thing. She teaches in a village school, she writes a clear hand, and she will be here at ten. I confess that the question interests me."

It was the morning of Tuesday, the third of November, 1896, and a raw yellow day pressed against the windows of Baker Street. At ten exactly the page announced Miss Ellen Dowsett.

She was a slight young woman of six- or seven-and-twenty, in a black dress which had been made over from an older one, with a pale, resolute face and very direct grey eyes. Holmes rose, set a chair for her, and regarded her for a moment with that air of languid attention which his friend had learned to distrust.

"You have walked a considerable distance upon wet sand this morning, Miss Dowsett," said he, "and you came up by the first train from Shoeburyness, and you have not yet had your breakfast. You teach the younger children, I think, and you have a cold room to do it in."

She looked at him in some astonishment, and then down at herself.

"That is perfectly true, every word of it. But how—"

"It is perfectly obvious. There is a line of grey salt upon your hem, three inches from the edge, which only a tide-flat leaves, and a little fine sand in the welt of your boot, still damp. A lady who crosses sand before dawn must catch the first train or none. There is chalk upon the side of your right forefinger, and none upon the left, from which I infer a slate rather than a blackboard; and your hands are chapped across the knuckles in a manner which I associate with a schoolroom stove that nobody lights before nine. Doctor, ring for some coffee and an egg. Now, Miss Dowsett, pray tell us your story from the beginning, and do not leave out anything because you think it too small."

She drew a breath, folded her chapped hands in her lap, and spoke for some time without stopping, in the low, even voice of a person who has rehearsed a thing many times alone.

"I teach the infants at the school on Foulness, Mr. Holmes. It is an island at the mouth of the Thames, on the Essex side, beyond Shoeburyness, and there is no bridge to it. At low water you may cross to the mainland by the sands. The track is called the Broomway, because it is marked out by brooms: poles set in the sand, with a bunch of twigs bound upside down at the top of each, so that they look like brooms standing on their handles. It runs for some miles along the sands, a good way out from the shore, and at certain points there are side-paths which go in to the sea wall of the island. We call those the heads. My father kept the brooms, and he drove the mail-cart. He took it over to Great Wakering and back on every tide that served, with the letters, and the parcels, and anybody who wanted to go. He had done it for one-and-thirty years. He said that he could have driven it blind, and I believe that he could.

"On Thursday, the twenty-second of October, he went over to Wakering in the afternoon, and he started back at the evening low water, after dark. It was a clear night when he started. A fog came in from the sea when he was half-way over. Such fogs come very suddenly upon us in the autumn. He did not come home. In the morning they found the cart in a gully a good quarter of a mile to the seaward of the track, off Rugwood Head, and the pony drowned in the shafts. They found my father on the second tide.

"There was an inquest at the inn, and the jury said that he had lost his way in the fog and turned too soon, and that it was misadventure. Everybody was very kind. Mrs. Belsham, who farms behind Rugwood Head, paid for the funeral, and would not hear of my paying her back. But I do not believe it, Mr. Holmes. He did not lose his way. He never went out in a fog in his life if he could help it. There was only one time that he would not go, and that was in the winter of 'eighty-six, when he was asked to fetch the doctor over through a thick fog at night, for a child that was ill. He would not go. He said that he would drown the pony and himself and bring no doctor, and I daresay he was right; but the child died before morning, and my father never forgave himself. After that he watched the sky like a sailor. And yet this fog came upon him in the middle of the sands, and he turned off the brooms, which he knew one by one, as I know the faces of my children.

"There is one thing more. My brother Tom is seventeen. He was coming down to the wall at Rugwood Head that night with a lantern to meet the cart, as he often did. He told the coroner that he saw a light out upon the sands, a good way off, to the right of the track, before the fog thickened, and that it stood still. The coroner said that it was my father's own lantern, and that he must have been off the track already. But Tom says it was not moving, and my father's lamp was always moving, because the cart was. Tom has the carrying now, and the mail with it. He crosses every tide. The tides are coming round to the dark again this week. That is the whole of it, sir, and I daresay it is nothing. Can a man be drowned by a road, Mr. Holmes?"

Holmes had listened with his eyes closed and his finger-tips together. He opened them now.

"Not by the road, Miss Dowsett. By the man who moves it, possibly; that is the only explanation which fits the facts as you give them. Did your father ever speak of the brooms being disturbed?"

"Never. The tide breaks one now and then, and he set a new one."

"And the child of 'eighty-six. Whose child was it?"

She hesitated.

"I was sixteen, and away at the training school. I never knew which house it was. Father would not speak of it."

"It is of no consequence for the moment." He sprang up and began to pace the room. "When does your brother next cross after dark?"

"The evening tides fall later each day. By Friday he will be bringing the mail over at night."

"Then we have until Friday." He stopped at the window. "Doctor, if a man knows a road so well that he can drive it blind, how is he to be lost upon it?"

"If the road is changed," said the doctor.

"Precisely. That is the obvious inference. And if the marks of a road can lie, what guide is there that cannot?"

The doctor did not know, and said so.

"Nor do I, at this moment. But I know a man who might be one. Miss Dowsett, will you go home by the afternoon train and say nothing whatever of this visit to anybody: not to your brother, and not to the kind Mrs. Belsham? We shall come down on Thursday."

When she had gone he sat down at the table and wrote a short note, which he blotted, read twice, and handed to his friend in an envelope.

"I want you to carry that to No. 7, Savile Row, Doctor, this afternoon, and give it into the hand of the gentleman there. He will receive you standing, and he will tell you how many minutes he can spare you. I advise you not to waste one of them."

"And if he asks me what it is about?"

"He will not ask. That is the whole of his value."

The door in Savile Row was opened by a round, pink, cheerful little man in a black coat, who looked at the doctor's card, and then at the doctor, with an expression of the liveliest interest.

"The friend of Monsieur Holmes!" said he. "The doctor who writes. Come in, sir. Monsieur is at home until half past four."

He showed the visitor into a study so orderly that it seemed to have been arranged that morning and never touched since. A white-haired gentleman stood beside the writing-table with his watch open in his hand. He was tall and spare, very upright, with a fine, still, colourless face, and he looked at the doctor as a man looks at a clock which he has reason to think is fast.

"I can give you eleven minutes," said he.

The doctor gave him the note. He read it in a single glance. The doctor was afterwards permitted to copy it, and it is given here.

Dear Mr. Fogg,—A man who had crossed the Maplin Sands for one-and-thirty years was drowned upon them in fog on the night of the 22nd ult., a quarter of a mile off his road. I believe that the marks of that road were moved. His son must cross it at night on Friday next. In 1881 I counted your paces between the lamps, and they did not vary. I want a man who can keep a line and a minute upon open sand without a single mark to help him. I know of no other. I go down by the 2.15 from Fenchurch Street on Thursday.—S. H.

Mr. Fogg folded it and laid it on the table.

"Was he found?" said he.

"On the second tide," said the doctor. "His daughter told us so."

He made no remark upon this. He crossed to a shelf, took down a small thick book in a paper cover, which the doctor saw to be a table of the tides, and turned its leaves with a long, steady finger. Then he took up a pencil, wrote a few figures upon the margin of the blotter, looked at them, and appeared to find them satisfactory.

"Low water off Foulness on Friday evening falls at about twenty minutes past seven," said he. "The sands will be bare from soon after five until near ten. The sun sets before half past four. It will be dark." He looked up. "There is no moon."

"Then you will come?"

He did not answer. He drew towards him a small green book which lay upon the blotter, opened it, wrote one line in a square, unhurried hand, and turned the book so that the doctor could read it, as a man might show a receipt.

3 Nov. That I shall stand at the foot of Rugwood Head, Foulness, by count, at 7h 46m p.m. on the 6th.

"You may tell Mr. Holmes," said he, "that I shall be at Fenchurch Street at fourteen minutes past two."

It was the most singular acceptance of an invitation that the doctor had ever received; but he had been warned, and he asked nothing. The little steward, who had lingered at the door, came forward and coughed.

"Monsieur will take a lantern," said he, "and the thick boots, and the coat with the deep pockets. And Monsieur will take this." He drew from his waistcoat an enormous silver watch, as large as a small turnip, and held it out upon his palm. "It is a family watch, sir," he said to the doctor, since his master was not looking at it. "It was my great-grandfather's. It has gone round the whole world in my pocket, and I have never once put it on or back. It keeps London time. Upon the sand there is water. If the watch of Monsieur takes the water, this one shall not, because I shall sew it into an oilskin."

Mr. Fogg looked at the watch, and then at his steward, and for a moment there passed over his face something which in another man the doctor would have called a smile. It was gone before he was sure of it.

"Sew it, then," said Mr. Fogg. He closed his own watch. "Your eleven minutes are up, Doctor. Good afternoon."

The doctor walked back to Baker Street through the dusk with one question going round in his head, and when he put it to Holmes his friend only laughed.

"Why should a gentleman of four-and-sixty leave his rubber for a carrier he never saw, upon the word of a man he has met three times? I do not know, my dear fellow. The facts admit of more than one explanation, and nobody has paid me to find out."

"You might ask him."

"I might," said Holmes. "I doubt that I should be answered."

At Fenchurch Street on the Thursday the clock stood at fourteen minutes past two when a tall white-haired gentleman in a long dark coat came through the barrier with a lantern in one hand and a small bag in the other, and took his seat in the compartment which Holmes had kept for him. He bowed to Holmes, and to the doctor, and said nothing else whatever.

The doctor, looking back along the platform as the train drew out, observed a curious thing. A small, slight, elderly man, very neatly dressed, stood by the barrier without a ticket, writing in a notebook; and his eyebrows, which were thick and grey, twitched up and down as though they had a life of their own. He did not get into the train. He looked at the clock, wrote again, and walked away; and the doctor thought no more of him for thirteen years.

They ran down beside the river through a flat grey country of marsh and dyke, and beyond Southend the guns at Shoeburyness were firing out over the sands. Mr. Fogg sat upright with his hands upon his stick and watched the water.

At Shoeburyness a hired trap took them two miles and a half inland to Great Wakering, where a burly, slow, red-faced constable was waiting for them at the door of the inn with a telegram in his hand and doubt in every line of his face.

"Inspector says I'm to give you every assistance, sir," said he to Holmes. "Ketley's my name. I don't rightly see what assistance there is to give. Dowsett was drowned, and the jury said so."

"Then you shall see him drowned again," said Holmes cheerfully, "and tell me afterwards whether the jury was right."

At Wakering Stairs, in the last of the light, the land simply stopped. Beyond it lay a waste of mud and sand the colour of a slate, and very far off, to the left, the low dark line of the sea wall of Foulness. Holmes stopped to read a notice-board at the top of the Stairs, which warned the public, by order of the War Department, not to touch any shell found upon the sands. Nobody else looked at it.

Tom Dowsett was waiting at the bottom with a pony and a light cart, and the mail-bags under a tarpaulin. He was a tall, fair, silent lad, with his sister's grey eyes, and he had plainly been told nothing, and as plainly had guessed a great deal.

"Tide's right, sir," he said. "We go now or we don't go."

They went. A causeway took them across soft black mud to hard ribbed sand, and the track swung round to the left and away along the flats, a quarter of a mile from the shore. The brooms stood upon one hand only, each a stout pole with a bush of brushwood bound to the top, going away ahead of the cart, smaller and smaller, into the dusk.

Mr. Fogg did not get into the cart. He walked beside the pony's head with his lantern, and the doctor, who was riding upon the mail-bags, presently perceived that he was counting. He counted from one broom to the next, and wrote nothing down. At the fourth broom he took a paper from his pocket, which the doctor saw to be a sheet of the Ordnance map that Holmes had bought in the Strand, held it to the lantern for a moment, and put it back.

"How far apart do they stand, Tom?" said Holmes.

"Thirty yards, sir, near enough. There's nigh on four hundred of 'em."

"Thirty-six of my paces," said Mr. Fogg, "upon sand."

They waded at the mouth of a creek, with the water to the pony's knees and running fast, and Mr. Fogg walked through it without breaking his count, holding his lantern high. Darkness came down. Then far ahead, on the dark line of the island, a red spark appeared, and grew, and became a fire.

"That's Churchend, sir," said Tom, with a kind of pride. "That's the Fifth. They burn the guy on the wall every year, and we steer for it, coming home."

"There will be no fire to-morrow," said Holmes.

"No, sir."

Mr. Fogg glanced at the fire, glanced at the brooms, and walked on. At the end of nearly two hours the cart turned in at a short line of brooms which went up to the island, and a post with an arm loomed out of the dark with RUGWOOD HEAD painted upon it in white. Mr. Fogg stopped beneath it, looked at his watch, and made a small mark with a pencil upon his cuff.

"Two hundred and sixty brooms from the Stairs," said he, "and a creek. I shall know the rest to-morrow."

The doctor fell back beside Holmes as they climbed the head to the wall.

"Holmes, it is extraordinary. Where in the world does a man learn to do that?"

"In the same place, I imagine, where he learns not to answer the question," said Holmes. "It is a singular kind of school."

The inn at Churchend had two bedrooms, and the constable had one of them. The difficulty was settled before it arose by a tall, handsome, dark-browed woman of five-and-forty, who came along the wall with a lantern while the guy was still burning, took Miss Dowsett's hand in both of hers, and then turned to the strangers with the frank hospitality of the country.

"I'm Mrs. Belsham, from the farm behind the head," said she. "Ellen tells me you're friends of her father's from London. You'll not sleep three in one room at the George. I've beds aired, and a fire, and nobody in the house but me and the girl."

She gave them supper in a great low kitchen, and talked as she served it; and she spoke of the island as a man speaks of a hard master whom he respects.

"You'll have heard the sands called a road, gentlemen. It's no road. It's the sea's own floor, and the sea lends it to us twice a day, and takes it back when it pleases. The sands take whom they like. They took my husband's father, and two girls in my grandmother's day, and a shepherd that followed a light. Isaac Dowsett knew them better than any man living, and they took him. No man on Foulness goes out upon them in the dark if he can help it. There's old stories of lights, and of voices calling, which were only somebody calling to draw a man off. We keep to our walls at night, and we let them call." She set down a dish. "And now it's young Tom must go out in the dark, because the letters can't wait for daylight. When does he bring the mail over to-morrow, poor lad? I always forget the tides, though I was born here."

"Low water falls at nineteen minutes past seven," said Mr. Fogg, "or within two minutes of it."

She looked at him with a sudden, quick attention, and then laughed.

"You're very exact, sir."

"I have a table," said Mr. Fogg.

After supper the doctor walked down to the George for tobacco, and found at the kitchen fire a big, fair, grey-headed fellow of sixty, the mildest-looking giant he had ever seen, whom they called the smith. Somebody asked him for a song, and he gave it, beating the time softly upon his knee with a fist like a mallet: "Beat it out, beat it out—Old Clem! With a clink for the stout—Old Clem!"

"That's a forge song," said the doctor.

"It were my father's, sir. He had the forge on the Kentish side, out upon the marshes over the river. You can see the Kentish shore from our wall of a clear day, and I look at it, I do. Which I meantersay, a man don't forget his marshes." He drank, and wiped his mouth. "I were named for a gentleman, sir. He were my father's 'prentice once, and went off and was a gentleman, and come back. And what larks we had, when he come."

The doctor stayed a little, and as he went out by the churchyard he held up his lantern, idly, to the nearest stones. One of them was small, and very clean, and had a little iron railing round it, freshly painted black. The moss had got into the lower letters. He made out David, only son of John and Hannah B——, and below, aged 8 years, 14th January, 1886.

"I made that rail, sir," said a slow voice at his shoulder; the smith had come out behind him. "In the January of 'eighty-six. Croup, it were, in the night, and a fog. His mother paints it every spring."

The doctor told Holmes of this when he came back, and of the song, and of the smith; and Holmes, who was sitting by the kitchen fire with his long legs stretched out and his eyes half closed, heard him to the end without moving.

"The fourteenth of January," said he at last. "A night of fog, a child with croup, and a carrier who would not go. It is suggestive, Doctor; it is very suggestive. What is the Christian name of the woman who gave us our supper?"

"I have no idea."

"No," said Holmes. "Nor have I. I should be glad to know it."

The morning low water fell before sunrise. At a quarter to seven on the Friday, in a grey, still, bitter dawn, Holmes, the doctor, Mr. Fogg and the constable went down Rugwood Head to the sands, while Tom Dowsett drove the mail-cart away past them towards Wakering and diminished along the line of brooms into the haze.

Holmes went to work at once. He walked along the main line towards Wakering, stooping at each broom, and at the fourth from the turning he went down upon his knees on the wet sand and remained there so long that the constable began to stamp his feet.

"You see it, Doctor?"

The doctor bent over him. The pole of the broom was black and green with weed from the sand upwards, as the others were; but between the sand and the beginning of the green there was a band of pale, clean wood a couple of inches deep, as white as a peeled stick.

"It is the same with the next," said Holmes, "and the next, and the one after. Four brooms. The evidence is perfectly clear. The rest have the weed down to the sand. Well, Doctor? You have the medical eye. Why should four brooms out of four hundred wear a white collar?"

The doctor considered it.

"Weed grows where the light and the water reach it," said he slowly. "It does not grow under the sand. Then, clearly, the white part was under the sand until lately. These four have been pulled up and set in again, and not so deep as before."

"Excellent! That is a remarkable observation, Doctor, and precisely the point. Pulled up, carried, set in again: and lately, as we may deduce, for a month of tides will put the green upon it. Mr. Ketley, the sea will break a broom and wash it away. Did you ever know it draw one two feet out of the sand, and put it back two inches shallower?"

The constable rubbed his chin and said that he had not.

Mr. Fogg had said nothing. He had walked down the line beyond the four brooms and back again, and now he stood by the turning with his watch in his hand.

"They stand at their intervals," said he. "Thirty-six paces each. Whoever put them back put them back in their own holes, or very near."

"Then where were they in the meantime?" said the doctor.

"That," said Holmes, "is the question for to-night. Look about you." He swept his arm round at the sands. "Landward, the wall. Seaward, that gully, where the water runs first and the mud is soft; you can see it shine. A man who comes along this line in a fog sees one broom, and then the next. If the next is not where it should be, but over there, towards the gully, he goes to it, and to the one beyond it, and he goes on looking for the fifth. There is no fifth. And the tide comes in behind him up that gully, and the fog is all round him, and he has lost all notion of north and south."

The constable looked at the gully and said nothing at all. Already the water was moving in it, quick and silent, for the flood had begun to make; and they went back up the head before it reached the hollows. From the wall Mr. Fogg looked for a long time out to sea, to the east, where a low grey bank lay along the horizon like a second shore.

"The glass stands very high," said he. "There will be fog upon the flats by three o'clock."

"To-night, then," said Holmes.

The fog came in at ten minutes to three. It came off the sea in a soft grey wall, without a breath of wind, and by half past three the farmhouse might have stood alone in the world.

The doctor spent the afternoon sewing a lantern into a frame of sacking, so that it would show a light upon one side only. Mr. Fogg sat by the window with three watches open upon the sill: his own, the doctor's, and the steward's great silver turnip in its oilskin; and every half hour he compared them. Once, while Holmes was out in the yard with the constable, he opened the little green book upon the sill and wrote a line beneath the entry of Tuesday; and the doctor, who sat nearest, saw it: That I shall be on the sea wall by 8h 0m.

At five Mrs. Belsham came in from the yard in a long cloak, with a lantern.

"I'm going along the wall to see to my ewes," said she. "There's no seeing anything in this, and they'll be down by the saltings. I'll be back for supper." She looked round at them, and her eyes rested a moment on the watches. "And Tom will be on the sands by now, poor lad."

"Not until five minutes past six, Mrs. Belsham," said Mr. Fogg. "He is to leave the Stairs by my watch."

When the door had shut behind her, the doctor turned to Holmes in amazement.

"Holmes, you will let her go out?"

"I will let anybody go out," said Holmes. "It is her farm, and it is quite possible that she is going to her ewes. Now listen to me, all of you, for we have very little time. Doctor, at seven you will take Mr. Ketley and the smith, and the smith will take his anvil to the wall at the top of Rugwood Head in a barrow. He has promised it to me. At ten minutes to eight by your watch, which Mr. Fogg has set, he will strike it three times; and three times every minute after, until we come up. Whatever you hear, do not come down upon the sands. If a light comes up the head, it will be Tom and the cart. Keep him there."

"And you?"

"Mr. Fogg and I go down the head at twenty-five minutes to eight. We shall be at the foot of it, on the main line, at fourteen minutes to eight. Tom should reach the turning a few minutes later." He turned. "You have the times, Mr. Fogg?"

"Low water at twenty minutes past seven. The cart walks two miles and a half in the hour. It will be at the foot of the head at nine minutes to eight."

"And the flood?"

"The gully begins to run soon after eight, and it fills fast. The line is bare until half past nine."

"How far do you trust your count, Mr. Fogg?"

Mr. Fogg closed the three watches one after another.

"Further than the brooms," said he.

They went down the head into a darkness so thick that the lantern showed nothing but its own yellow ball of fog, and the wet sand at their feet. The short line of brooms which led down from the wall came at them one by one out of the grey, and went behind them. Mr. Fogg walked a little in front, with the lantern held low and his compass in his other hand; and Holmes, at his shoulder, heard him counting under his breath.

At the foot of the head the brooms of the main line began. Mr. Fogg turned upon them towards Wakering, and stopped, and looked at the watch in his hand.

"Fourteen minutes to eight," said he; and wrote nothing.

They went on along the line. One broom; thirty-six paces; the next. Thirty-six; the next. At the third Mr. Fogg checked, and held the lantern forward. There was no broom beyond it. There was only wet sand, ribbed and shining, and the fog.

"The gap," said Holmes softly. "They have been at it again."

Mr. Fogg did not answer. He looked down at his compass, lifted his head, and walked on into the empty sand exactly as though the brooms were there. Thirty-six, and nothing. Seventy-two, and nothing. A hundred and eight. A hundred and forty-four. At a hundred and eighty a broom stood up out of the fog straight in front of him, with the weed upon it down to the sand.

"Four are gone," said Mr. Fogg, "and this one stands in its place."

Holmes had swung the lantern to the seaward. Away there, dim in the grey, there stood a broom where no broom should be; and beyond it, fainter still, another, leaning a little away towards the gully.

"There is the false road," said he, "and there"—he caught Mr. Fogg's arm—"there is the light that stood still."

Far out in the fog, low down, a yellow smudge of light hung motionless.

"Five minutes, Mr. Fogg. I must see who holds it."

"If you leave the line," said Mr. Fogg, "you will not find it again. The gully begins to run at eight."

"I shall go by the false brooms. They lead to her."

"They lead into the gully. That is what they are for."

But Holmes was gone. For a moment the sacking lantern showed his long figure stooping from one false broom to the next, and then the fog took the light and the man together, and Mr. Fogg was alone upon the sands.

He stood quite still. He took the bearing upon which Holmes had gone, and wrote it upon his cuff; then he set his lantern down at the foot of the true broom, and waited. At eight minutes to eight there came a creak of wheels and the splash of hoofs, and a second light swam out of the fog upon the line. Tom Dowsett was walking at the pony's head. He pulled up short at the sight of a white-haired gentleman standing beside a broom with a watch in his hand, as if he were waiting for a train.

"Do not turn off to the right, Tom, whatever brooms you see. Keep straight upon the line by this compass, here, at the mark. Thirty-six of my paces is forty of yours. Count a hundred and eighty across the gap; then you will find three brooms, and the head, and go up it. At the top you will find the doctor. Go."

The lad went without a word. Mr. Fogg watched his light go on into the grey and vanish. Then, very far off upon the wall above, there came three clear strokes of iron upon iron, and after them nothing.

"Ten minutes to eight," said Mr. Fogg; and he turned to the seaward, upon the bearing on his cuff, and began to count.

He counted aloud now. The sand ran down into a hollow, and the hollow was full of water, cold and quick, and moving. He went through it. At a hundred and twenty paces the water was at his knees. At a hundred and fifty the bottom fell away under him, and he went in to the waist, and stood, and held the lantern up, and came out upon the other side. He took out his watch. It had stopped at four minutes to eight. He put it back, took from his inner pocket the steward's oilskin, and opened it; and the great silver watch lay there in its wrapping, ticking loudly in the silence, at London time.

The anvil sounded again behind him, three strokes, fainter now.

"Mr. Holmes!"

"Here!" The answer came from the right, close by, and a little behind. "Here, Mr. Fogg! Bear to your right. I am held."

He found them twenty paces off. Holmes stood up to his knees in water upon a bank of mud which shook under him, and he had both arms locked about the body of a woman who hung against him with her head fallen back. Her legs were in the mud to the knee. Her lantern lay upon its side beside her, still alight, with the water lapping at it.

"She went in at the end of the false road," said Holmes between his teeth, "and the mud took her. I cannot get her out alone. If I let go she is under in five minutes."

Mr. Fogg set down his lantern, put the silver watch between his teeth, and took the woman under the other arm. They pulled together. There was a long, sucking reluctance, and a cry from her; and then she came out of it all at once, and left one boot behind her in the mud, and the three of them went down together into the water.

They got up. Mr. Fogg took the watch out of his mouth and looked at it.

"Eleven minutes," said he. "Not more. Bring her."

They brought her between them. Mr. Fogg walked in front with the lantern, upon the reverse of his bearing, and he counted backwards now, from the number at which he had found them, down to nothing; and through the gully, where the water was above the waist and pulling hard at their legs, he did not alter his count by a single pace. At nought, the true broom stood up before them out of the fog, with his own lantern still burning at its foot.

"The head is three brooms beyond the gap," said he.

From somewhere above them in the dark, three strokes of the anvil fell upon the fog; and with them, quite clear and slow, as if the smith had forgotten everything but his work, came the burden of the old song: "Beat it out, beat it out—Old Clem!" They turned towards it, and went up the head with the water running out of their clothes and the gullies loud behind them.

Upon the wall the doctor had stood with his watch open in his hand, calling the minutes to the smith, who struck his three strokes and stared into the fog with the patience of an ox. At eight o'clock exactly a light had come up the head: the cart, and Tom Dowsett, as white as paper. After that there was nothing to do but wait, and listen to the water coming in.

At a quarter past eight the smith said that he could hear the gullies running. At twenty past the constable said that no man who was off the line could get back across them now. At half past eight the smith stopped in the middle of a stroke and lifted his great head; and out of the fog below came the sound of men breathing hard, and splashing, and three figures came up the head together, the middle one carried between the other two.

The constable stepped forward with his lantern. Its light fell upon the face of the woman, and he stopped, and his jaw dropped.

"Mrs. Belsham!"

"Hannah Belsham," said Holmes, "of the farm behind this head. Mr. Ketley, I give her into your charge for the wilful murder of Isaac Dowsett upon the night of the twenty-second of October; and you will find, at the morning tide, at the end of four brooms which have no business where they stand, the boot in which she went out to-night to drown his son."

She had got her breath now. She stood up between the two men, swaying, and she did not deny it. She looked at Holmes with a strange, still expression, as though she had been relieved of something.

"He told you, did he? The little rail," said she. "Well, it's no matter. I'll tell it myself, and you'll write it down right. It was a night like this, in the January of 'eighty-six. My Davy had the croup. He was eight years old, and he couldn't get his breath, and he went black in the face, and I sat up with him till midnight, and then I went down to Isaac Dowsett's door. I asked him to take the cart over for the doctor. He said no. He said no man went upon the sands in the dark and the fog, and he'd drown his pony and himself and fetch no doctor. I went on my knees to him in the road. He shut the door. Davy died at four in the morning, with his hand in mine; and I've walked past Isaac Dowsett's door every day for ten years, and given him good-day. I waited ten years for a fog at a dark tide with him out upon the sands. It came on the twenty-second. I had the four brooms out and set before he came, and I stood with my lamp at the end of them, so that he'd think it was the head. And when he'd gone by me I put them back. Nobody saw me, but the lad. He saw my lamp, and he said so at the inquest, and they all said it was his father's; but I knew he'd think of it again some day. A lad that sees a light remembers it." She turned her face towards the doctor. "You're a doctor, they tell me. Would he have lived, my Davy, if Isaac had gone?"

The doctor did not answer at once.

"Mrs. Belsham, what you describe is clearly membranous croup, which is diphtheria of the throat," said he gently. "Two years ago a serum was found for it, and children live now who would have died. But in 'eighty-six it was impossible for any doctor in England to have saved your son: not across the sands, and not in Harley Street. If Isaac Dowsett had gone, he would have come back with a doctor to watch the boy die."

She heard him out. Then she put her two hands over her face and stood so; and the constable took her gently by the arm and led her away along the wall into the fog, with the smith going before them with the lantern. The doctor heard the smith's slow voice comforting her, as one comforts a child.

Holmes stood looking after them. He was soaked to the breast and shaking with cold, and his face in the lantern-light was grey.

"I owe you my life, Mr. Fogg," said he, "and an apology for my method, which was wrong. It is not often that I say either."

"You owe me nothing," said Mr. Fogg. "You were wrong by thirty-one minutes."

Late that night, in the kitchen of the George, the doctor sat up by the fire, unable to sleep. Holmes had gone with the constable to the room where the prisoner lay. Mr. Fogg sat opposite in borrowed clothes, with his boots steaming upon the fender and his own stopped watch upon the table beside the steward's going one. Presently he took the little green book from the breast of his coat, which hung drying upon a chair, and opened it, and wrote; and then, as before, he turned it so that the doctor could see.

6 Nov. Won. 7h 46m.

That I shall be on the sea wall by 8h 0m.

Lost. 8h 31m. Cause: Venn's guest.

The doctor read it twice.

"And who is Venn?" said he.

"A gentleman who brought a guest to my club," said Mr. Fogg, "in the October of 1881." He closed the book. "It was the same guest."

At the morning low water the sands lay bare and shining under a clear cold sky, as though there had never been a fog in the world. The constable stood at the end of the four false brooms with his arms folded, and a face in which the doubt of Thursday was fighting a hard rear-guard action.

"I'll not say you're wrong, sir," said he. "But a jury's a jury. She was on the sands, and she was in the mud, and she says now that she did it. She may say otherwise to-morrow, with a lawyer at her elbow. A woman may go out after her ewes."

"In a fog, at a dark tide, to the end of a false road which she has made?" said Holmes. "Very well, Mr. Ketley. You shall have it from the sand, and not from me, and then she may say what she pleases. Pull up that broom."

The constable took hold of the nearest of the four, worked it to and fro, and drew it out of the sand with a sucking sound. He held it up. Above the black, dripping foot of the pole were two bands of pale wood, one above the other, the lower just beginning to take the green.

"Two collars," said Holmes. "One from the twenty-second of October, and one from last night. A broom that the sea has broken has no collar at all. A broom that has stood since the spring has none either. These four have been pulled twice in a fortnight, and they are the only four upon the whole road. Now walk to the end of them."

The constable walked to the end of the false line, where the sand ran down into the gully. In the soft mud at the edge, a little sunk, there was something dark. He stooped and dragged it out. It was a woman's boot, strong and well greased, with the laces still tied.

"It will fit the one she has on," said Holmes. "And now let me give it you in its order, for I see that the doctor is anxious to have it, and he will have to write it down.

"Miss Dowsett came to Baker Street with a story which had only one peculiar thing in it. Her father, who could drive this road blind, was lost upon it. A man who knows a road cannot be lost on it unless the road is altered; that is evident; and the only part of this road which can be altered is the brooms. So I came to look at the brooms. Four of them, at a single place, had been pulled and set in again within the month. That was the method; and the method indicated a good deal about the person. It must be someone who could be upon these sands in a fog at a dark low water, and back upon the wall before the flood, and not be seen: someone, therefore, who lived close by this head. It must be someone strong enough to draw a pole that is sunk two feet, and four of them, and twice in a single tide. And it must be someone who knew that Isaac Dowsett would be upon the sands that night, when every islander keeps to his wall after dark.

"Then there was the light which Tom Dowsett saw. The coroner said that it was his father's lamp. But the father's lamp was upon a moving cart, and this light stood still, off the head, near the line. Why should a light stand still upon the sands in a rising fog? There is only one reason. It was there to be steered for. It was the head which a lost man would look for; it was a false head at the end of a false road. And it followed that the person who held it knew that the son had seen it, for the son said so, before the whole parish, at the inquest. It followed also that the son was in danger whenever the fog came again at a dark tide with him upon the sands. Miss Dowsett told us that the tides were coming round to the dark this very week. That is why we came down on Thursday.

"There remained the reason. Isaac Dowsett had no money and no enemies, and he had done only one thing in his life which a reasonable person could hold against him. His daughter gave us the fact without knowing its value. In the winter of 'eighty-six he would not cross in a fog at night to fetch a doctor to a sick child, and the child died. She did not know which child. The doctor found the stone in the churchyard: a boy of eight, dead upon the fourteenth of January, 1886, with a little railing round him which his mother paints every spring. The name upon it was half hidden; but the smith had made the rail, and the smith knew the mother. And on Thursday evening, at her own table, the mother asked Mr. Fogg at what hour young Tom would bring the mail over; and Mr. Fogg, who answers exactly every question that is put to him, told her to the minute. She went out at five to see to her ewes. Her ewes, Mr. Ketley, are in the fold behind the farm, where they have been all the week.

"I made one error in the whole affair, and it was not an error of reasoning. I should have let Mr. Fogg keep the line, and taken her upon the wall at her return, with her four brooms upon the sand to speak for themselves. I went after the light, because it is my habit to go after things; and if Mr. Fogg had not the habit of counting, you would be looking this morning for two bodies instead of for a boot." He turned to his friend. "You may put that in, Doctor, if you are so good. I have remarked that you seldom do."

The constable looked at the boot in his hand, and at the broom with its two pale collars, and at the long empty line of the true road running away to Wakering; and he slowly put the boot into the pocket of his coat.

"I'll take her over to Rochford by the evening tide," said he.

Away along the line the mail-cart was coming down from the head, with Tom Dowsett at the pony's head and a tall, white-haired figure seated very straight upon the board beside the mail-bags. Mr. Fogg had a train to catch at Shoeburyness, and a rubber at eight. The cart did not slacken as it went by. Mr. Fogg raised his hat to them, once; and the cart went on along the brooms and dwindled away over the shining sand towards Wakering, carrying the island's letters to the mainland, which were, as the doctor afterwards learned, chiefly bills.
