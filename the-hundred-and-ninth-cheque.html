The Hundred and Ninth Cheque

February 1901
The Wager Ledger
An illustrated edition · Complete original story

A cheque for two hundred and fifty pounds had come to a house in Amsterdam four times a year for twenty-seven years. Each was dated on the day it fell due, and not one had come a day late. On Christmas Day, 1900, the hundred and ninth was due. It did not come.

The lady to whom it had not come sat in the visitor's chair at Baker Street on the morning of Friday, the first of February, 1901. She had crossed from Holland in the night. She was in black, but so was every woman in London that week. The Queen had been dead ten days. Tomorrow she would be drawn through the streets on a gun-carriage, and the drapers had sold crape to people who had never owned any.

Holmes was forty-seven. The doctor, who had shared the rooms again for nearly seven years, was told that he might stay.

She was a small, upright woman of about fifty, with a dark, fine face and an English a good deal purer than the doctor's. She laid four papers on the table in the order of their dates.

"I was married in London on the twenty-third of December, 1872. In the June following I went to my cousin in Holland for three months. My husband did not ask me to return. I did not return. That is all I shall say of it, Mr. Holmes, and I shall not give you his name. He is not the subject of the inquiry."

"Who is?"

"A solicitor in Bedford Row. On Christmas Day of 1873 his cheque came to me, with this."

Holmes read the first paper aloud.

…an annuity of one thousand pounds, purchased upon your life alone, and payable to you for the term of that life in four equal sums, upon the usual quarter-days…

"They are the four days on which the English pay their rent," said the lady. "The twenty-fifth of March, the twenty-fourth of June, the twenty-ninth of September, and Christmas Day. I have had occasion to learn them. The cheque has come a hundred and eight times. It is the solicitor's own, and it is dated the day itself. When the day was a Sunday, it was dated the Sunday. The first was dated on a Christmas Day, and so were twenty-six after it."

"And at Christmas last?"

"Nothing. I wrote. I have had three answers." "On the second of January, the season had delayed the office. On the fourteenth, the office was in arrear by reason of the war. On the twenty-fourth, the death of Her Majesty had suspended all business, and he begged my patience until the twenty-fifth of March."

"Three letters and three reasons," said Holmes. "What office?"

"He has never named it. I never asked."

"In twenty-seven years, has any person required you to prove that you were alive?"

"No."

"Who bought this for you?"

"My husband, I was told. Once, in 1873, and so made an end of it. I thought it very like him. It was a thing done correctly, a single time, and not thought of again." "I ought to tell you that I am the consequence of an error, Mr. Holmes. My husband once mistook the day of the week. He believed it was a Saturday, and it was a Friday. It is the only mistake I ever knew him to make, and but for it I should not have been his wife."

"Today is a Friday," said the doctor, who liked to be of use.

Holmes had sat up.

"Does your husband keep a small green book?"

The lady was on her feet before the doctor understood that anything had been said.

"I told you I should not name him."

"You have not. I named a book. Pray sit down. Why do you come now, and not on the third of January?"

"Because I was prevented from writing a fourth letter. My cousin's house has an old friend, a physician of Amsterdam, who has so many letters after his name that he ends them with an et cetera. He told me that I had asked a lawyer three times, and that I had not yet asked anybody who would answer. He was coming to London to see a pupil of his, who keeps a house for the insane at Purfleet, and I crossed under his care. He has a wife living who has not known his face for many years. He goes to see her. He says that he would change places with any person whose question could still be answered."

"When do you return?"

"Tomorrow night. The train for the Harwich boat leaves Liverpool Street at half past eight, and I shall be in it, with an answer or without one."

"Tomorrow there will not be a bank or an office open in London. You have given me one working day, madam, and it is past ten."

"'Upon your life alone.' You are alive; I have the evidence of my eyes. Then the one thing in the world that this payment cannot do is the thing it has done. What have you been receiving for twenty-seven years?"

In a court off Cornhill there is an old house with two names over the door, under the paint. The first floor was the Candlewick Life Office. Its actuary was sixty-five, with a stiff leg and a stick, and the last time he had had Holmes in the building, Holmes had been a second-hand bookseller.

"You look better alive," he said. "What have you brought me?"

He read the paper of 1873 at his window, with the stick hooked over his arm.

"'Upon her life alone.' Then it is the simplest contract we write. A sum down, and the office pays until she dies. It cannot be in arrear. An office that missed a quarter would be in the newspapers by Thursday and in the hands of a receiver by the Thursday after. And we should have troubled her four times a year for a certificate that she breathed. We are not sentimental about that."

"She has never been asked for one."

"No office wrote this, Mr. Holmes. I know the forms of every office in London. I have been declining to believe them for forty years." He turned the letter over. "Dated on Christmas Day. There is not an office in the City that draws a cheque on Christmas Day, and I should be sorry if there were. I was brought up to keep it, by an old gentleman who learned how very late and never missed it afterwards."

"Then what has she had?"

"Four presents a year. Somebody has put two hundred and fifty pounds into that lawyer's hand on the morning of every quarter-day since 1873. He has done it on Sundays, and he has done it on Christmas Day, when no bank in England would do it for him. So it goes by hand. The lawyer has passed it on and called it by a longer name."

"That is my reading."

"A man who can count, and who does not keep Christmas." The lame gentleman looked up. "I had one in this office in the April of '94, with a betting book. He would not tell a lie to win his own wager."

"I have named nobody."

"No more have I. But a man of that pattern does not miss a day in twenty-seven years and then miss one, Mr. Holmes. Before I asked why it had stopped, I should want to know that he was alive."

The doctor had been sent to Pall Mall, and he met them in Bedford Row at ten minutes to three.

"The hall porter says that the gentleman came in at twenty-two minutes to twelve, as he has done every day for thirty years. He is in the reading room now."

The lady had been told in the cab what the lame gentleman said. She had answered that in that case her husband was dead, since he had not been late for anything since the year of her marriage. She heard the doctor out without moving.

"Alive," she said. "Then he has stopped."

"Or has been stopped," said Holmes. "Let us hear the lawyer."

Mr. Lascombe was sixty-three, silver-haired and unhurried, with a mourning band on his sleeve and black-edged paper on his desk. He came round the desk to the lady. He remembered the room in which she had signed, and the month, and that it had rained. His clerks were putting on their coats. The office shut at four, and would not open until Monday.

Holmes gave him the lame gentleman's argument in six sentences.

"Mr. Holmes is perfectly right, and I am relieved to be rid of it," said Mr. Lascombe. "There is no office, madam. The word was mine. I was five-and-thirty, and I thought it tactful. I judged that a lady who had left a house would not care to be paid by it four times a year. A gentleman has sent me his own cheque on each quarter-day, and I have had the honour to pass it on."

"And at Christmas?" said the lady.

"At Christmas nothing reached me. I wrote to you of delay because I hoped that it was delay. My instructions of 1873 forbid me to apply to the source, and I have kept them to the letter. I cannot pay what I have not received. But I should be ashamed to let you travel on that. Allow me, as a private matter, to advance you fifty pounds."

"Thank you, no."

"Three letters gave three reasons," said Holmes, "and each of them was an office's. Now there is no office, and the reason is a gentleman. I observe that you are never the party in arrear."

"I am a pipe, Mr. Holmes. A pipe is not in arrear. It is empty." He turned to the lady. "After seven-and-twenty years, madam, the kindest heart may be forgiven for tiring. Sentiment is not a perpetuity."

"Sentiment is unpunctual, sir," said Holmes. "This never was."

"When do you return, madam?"

"Tomorrow night, at half past eight."

"Then I fear that London will do nothing for you before you go. Leave me your direction. If anything comes on the twenty-fifth of March, you shall have it by the next post."

In the street the lady put down her veil.

"He is alive, and it has stopped at his end. That is an answer, Mr. Holmes. I did not come for more."

"You have a lawyer's word for it. He has given you his word four times since Christmas, and no two of them agree. There is a man in London who could settle it in four seconds. He would look it up."

"You will not go to him with my name. I have asked him for nothing in twenty-seven years. I shall not begin by asking why he has ceased to pay me."

"Without your name, then."

"You may ask him what you would ask a bank clerk, and nothing else. And you will not tell him that I am in London."

"I never volunteer a client's whereabouts, madam. I have not yet learned how to refuse a direct question. If he asks me?"

"He will not ask," said the lady. "It is the one thing I can promise you about him."

On the Saturday morning the streets east of Regent Street were empty. Every shutter was up, and London had gone west to stand along the Queen's road. At six minutes past eleven Holmes rang at 7 Savile Row.

The steward opened to him. He was fifty-nine, round and pink, and he had once carried a letter to this gentleman's door.

Mr. Fogg received his visitor standing, with his watch in his hand. He was sixty-nine. He had put on a black coat that morning, as he had every morning for forty years, and for once he was in the fashion.

"I can give you twenty-four minutes."

"I have a question that a bank clerk could answer, and no bank is open. Did you draw a cheque on Christmas Day?"

Mr. Fogg did not ask why. He had the green ledger open in four seconds. He turned the book, and laid a finger beside an entry.

25 Dec. 1900. Cheque 109. £250. By hand, 8h 10m. That she will return before Lady Day. One sovereign.

Under it there was no second line.

Holmes had come for the first half of that entry. He stood and read the second half. Lady Day was the lawyers' name for the twenty-fifth of March.

"A hundred and nine," he said. "May I see the hundred and eighth?"

The pages went back three months.

29 Sept. 1900. Cheque 108. £250. By hand, 8h 10m. That she will return before Christmas. One sovereign.
Lost.

"And the first."

The pages went back twenty-seven years.

25 Dec. 1873. Cheque 1. £250. By hand, 8h 10m. That she will return before Lady Day. One sovereign.
Lost.

"And those between?"

"The same."

Holmes took his word for it. A hundred and eight times the form had not varied by a syllable, and the second line had not varied at all. In Bedford Row they had called it sentiment, and tired sentiment. Holmes looked down the column. It was a hundred and eight appointments, and one of the parties had kept every one to the minute.

"You have wagered a hundred and nine times that a lady will come back," he said. "Have you asked her?"

"No."

"It would shorten the odds."

Mr. Fogg looked at his watch.

"The cheque of Christmas Day. Was it paid?"

"On the twenty-eighth."

His bankers returned his paid cheques with the pass-book on the first of each month. He kept them in a drawer of the desk, in the order of their dates, as another man keeps letters. He took out the last of them, and at Holmes's request the one before it. Holmes turned them over.

The cheque of September had gone, like a hundred and seven before it, into an account that the lawyers kept for their clients' money. The cheque of December had not. Across the back, in the lawyer's own hand, it was made over to Tolworth and Quennell, of Throgmorton Street.

"Stockbrokers," said Holmes. "On the twenty-eighth of December the London and Globe gave out that it could not pay, and half Throgmorton Street was sending for money by the afternoon. It is written on the back of your cheque where yours went. Nobody reads the back of a cheque."

"I did not."

"May I take the two?"

"The ledger is not lent. Those are paid. You may have them until midnight."

"And a copy of the entry?"

"It will be at Baker Street by four."

Holmes put the cheques in his pocket-book. He had some minutes left, and he did not need them.

"You do not ask who wishes to know."

"No."

"Nor where the question comes from."

"You would not be at liberty to say."

"I am forbidden to carry a name to this house. Nobody thought to forbid my answering a question, if one were put to me."

Mr. Fogg consulted his watch again.

"It is twenty-eight minutes past eleven. You have two minutes, Mr. Holmes."

"I have nothing more to ask," said Holmes. "I had hoped that you might."

Mr. Fogg put the ledger in his pocket. At half past eleven he shut his door.

It was a cold, misty morning. He went by Regent Street, where there was nobody, and came down into Waterloo Place, where everybody was.

Pall Mall was filled from wall to wall. Every face in it was turned west, towards the Palace. Nobody was going anywhere. In twenty-eight years Mr. Fogg had arrived at each kerb at the moment when the road was clear. There was no such moment in Pall Mall that day.

He stopped at the four hundred and sixtieth pace.

Somewhere beyond his sight, a gun-carriage went by with a small coffin on it under a white pall. He saw none of it. He saw ten thousand backs. Then every hat in the street came off together, and after that nothing moved at all. He took off his own.

He had once walked a mile, of his own will, and not counted it. This was another matter. He stood among people who were not counting anything. He did not know for how long he stood, and that was new. He thought of a question that had been offered to him at twenty-eight minutes past eleven, and of two minutes that he had handed back unused.

When the street breathed again he found that he no longer had the number. He did not begin again at one. He walked the rest, and was at the Reform Club at four minutes past twelve.

Mr. Lascombe had taken two seats in a window in St. James's Street, at twenty-five guineas, and he did not reach Bedford Square until a quarter past two. His parlourmaid had put the visitors in the dining-room at one.

He came in with his hat in his hand, and he had been moved.

"I have seen her go by," he said, to nobody. "I was born in the year she came to the throne."

Then he saw who was at his table.

Holmes laid two cheques on the cloth, face downward.

"Two cheques, Mr. Lascombe, in one hand, three months apart. I will ask you to read the backs."

The lawyer did not touch them. He sat down slowly.

"You have been to him."

"I have been to his cheque-drawer. He keeps them in the order of their dates, and he has never turned one over. On Christmas morning his man put that cheque into your hand, in the hall of this house, at about half past eight. On the Thursday you had it still. On the Friday the Globe went down, and your brokers wanted a difference before the afternoon. You had a client's cheque in your pocket-book, drawn to yourself. You wrote four words on the back of it. I daresay you meant it for a week."

"I meant it until the twenty-fifth of March, Mr. Holmes, and I mean it now." The voice was as good as it had been in Bedford Row. "I have held that trust for twenty-seven years. Twenty-seven thousand pounds has gone through this hand, and not a shilling of it has stuck. I was caught for nine hundred in a single afternoon, by a company with a marquis in the chair. I borrowed two hundred and fifty, for eighty-seven days, from the only two people in England who could be relied upon not to notice. He has never once asked me whether she received it. She has never once asked me where it came from. In twenty-seven years I have not had one question from either of them. You will allow that it was a temptation of an unusual shape."

"It was a loan while you held your tongue," said Holmes. "A man who borrows a quarter writes, 'I am in difficulty; wait until March.' You wrote to her of an office. Yesterday the office was taken away from you, and in forty seconds you had found her another reason. The gentleman had sent nothing. That was not said to cover eighty-seven days, sir. A lady who believes that her husband has stopped does not write a fourth letter. She takes the half-past-eight train. The gentleman draws his cheque on every quarter-day for as long as he lives, and sends it round by hand to this house, and nobody asks. You were not borrowing a quarter. You were coming into an annuity."

Mr. Lascombe looked at the backs of the cheques, since he had been asked to.

"Is it true, sir?" said the lady.

He was some time in answering, and when he did it was the first thing he had said to her that was not well said.

"Not on Christmas Day, madam. Not when I wrote the first letter. By the third, I think it was."

"No magistrate sits today," said Holmes. "I cannot give you in charge before Monday, and on Monday the lady will be in Holland. I can hold you to nothing, Mr. Lascombe, except what you choose to write at that desk in the next ten minutes."

"I will not have a police court," said the lady. "I will have my cheque."

He wrote it, on his private account, and it took him longer than a cheque ought.

"Date it, if you please, as the others were dated."

He dated it the twenty-fifth of December, 1900.

Then Holmes dictated, and the lawyer wrote to a gentleman in Savile Row. The letter resigned the trust. It returned the instructions of 1873. It stated, in one line and without an adjective, that the payment of Christmas had lain thirty-nine days in the trustee's hands.

"He will read that?"

"In four seconds. Then he will enter it."

Mr. Lascombe blotted the sheet.

"And who is to carry it to her on the twenty-fifth of March?"

"That is no longer your difficulty," said Holmes.

In the square the lady stood for a moment with the cheque in her glove.

"He drew it. At ten minutes past eight on Christmas morning. He has not stopped."

"No."

"A man will write me a cheque four times a year for twenty-seven years, and never one line to go with it. Why, Mr. Holmes?"

"Madam, I first put that question to myself in June of 1887, and I have remarked several times since that nobody was paying me to answer it. You are the first. Part of the answer is coming to Baker Street at four."

The steward came by the mews behind Conduit Street, in sixteen minutes, which at fifty-nine he thought respectable. The step at Baker Street was empty. The boys who had kept it once were men now, and some of them were in South Africa.

He was shown up with an envelope in his hand, and he did not deliver it. A lady in black had risen from the chair by the fire.

"Madame," said the steward.

He had last seen her in the June of 1873, getting into a cab.

"You gave me away, once," said the lady.

"In Marylebone, madame. I have not been asked to do anything so important since."

Holmes took the envelope from him, since nobody else would, and gave it to her. The copy was in a square, unhurried hand.

25 Dec. 1900. Cheque 109. £250. By hand, 8h 10m. That she will return before Lady Day. One sovereign.

"There are a hundred and eight before it," said Holmes. "The words are the same, and only the days are changed. Under each of them he has written 'Lost.' The book is not lent, madam, so you have only my word."

She read the line again. It was the first writing of his that she had seen since 1873, and it had been copied out for a stranger. For twenty-seven years she had believed in a thing done once.

"I do not read the book, madame; it is not my affair," said the steward. "But there is a thing I can tell you. Four nights in the year he comes home at midnight, as on every night, and he gives me his hat, and he asks me one question. 'Has anyone called?' Only on those four nights. I say, 'No, monsieur,' and he goes up. I have said it a hundred and eight times. I thought it was a habit of the English, on rent days."

The doctor looked out of the window.

"You will not tell him that I am here," said the lady.

"Madame!"

"He was offered the question this morning, and he looked at his watch. You will promise me."

"And if he asks?"

"Then you may answer him. I am told it is the rule of this house."

"It is not a quarter-day, madame. He will not ask before the twenty-fifth of March."

"Then on the twenty-fifth of March you may tell him that I called."

The steward promised, like a man lying down across a railway line. It was twenty minutes past four, and her train left in four hours and ten minutes.

The card room of the Reform Club had been shut that day by order of the committee. There were no evening papers, for none had been printed. Mr. Fogg sat in the reading room, in the chair that commanded the clock, with a sovereign on the Cape bulletin for eight o'clock.

At eight minutes to eight he was fetched into the hall. His steward stood on the mat with his hat in both hands.

"Is the house on fire?"

"No, monsieur."

"Then it will keep until midnight."

"At midnight it is of no use, monsieur. I have made a promise. I may not tell monsieur a thing. I may only answer."

"Answer what?"

"Monsieur knows what he asks me, four nights in the year."

"It is not a quarter-day."

"No, monsieur. Ask me all the same."

Mr. Fogg stood in the hall of his club for some seconds. He had been offered this once already that day. The hall porter, who had seen him stand so over a card, looked away.

"Has anyone called?"

"Not at the house, monsieur. At Baker Street. She goes from Liverpool Street at half past eight, by the train for the Harwich boat."

"A cab."

"It is at the door, monsieur. Since half past seven."

At four minutes to eight the Cape bulletin went up on the board. Nobody looked to see whether Mr. Fogg had won. He was not in the building.

Liverpool Street was full of people going home from a funeral. The Harwich train stood at its platform with the doors open and the lamps lit.

The physician from Amsterdam was a square, shaggy old man of seventy, with a black bag that he would trust to no porter. He had put the lady's rugs into the corner seat himself. He called the doctor "friend" on an acquaintance of nine minutes.

The lady gave Holmes an envelope.

"Your fee, Mr. Holmes. I asked whether a payment had stopped. You have told me that, and another thing. I have not had the answer to the question I put to you in the square."

"Nobody has, madam."

It was twenty-seven minutes past eight when the doctor touched his arm.

A white-haired gentleman was coming down the platform. He did not run. He had not the habit, and he had three minutes, which was sufficient. He stopped at the open door and took off his hat.

"Madam."

"Mr. Fogg."

They had not spoken since the June of 1873.

"Is our marriage still agreeable to you?"

He had put that question to her once before, in those words, on the evening of the twenty-first of December, 1872. It was the only question he had ever asked her. He had not found another in the interval.

The guard had his whistle at his lips. The old physician had got down again and was standing square in the doorway of the next compartment, with his bag held before him.

"Friend guard, one little minute. I am old, and my bag, he is in the wrong carriage."

The guard looked at the clock, and at the bag, and at the clock.

"In 1872 I answered you at once," said the lady. "You will allow me until the twenty-fifth of March, Mr. Fogg. It is a Monday."

She got in. The old man found that his bag was in the right carriage after all, and thanked the guard for his great patience, and the door was shut on him. The train left Liverpool Street at thirty-one minutes past eight.

Mr. Fogg had his watch in his hand. He looked at it, and for once he did not say what it told him.

"Well, Holmes," said the doctor. "You have been paid for that question at last."

"For a hundred and nine lines of it. There are some thousands in that book which are not about her."

Mr. Fogg had turned round.

"Mr. Holmes. To what address is the next to go?"

Holmes gave him a card.

"She left it with me this afternoon, for anybody who asked."

Mr. Fogg had now asked for something three times in one evening. Holmes thought the practice was growing on him.

He came in at midnight. The steward took his hat. It was not a quarter-day, and Mr. Fogg did not ask whether anyone had called.

In the study he opened the green ledger at the day's page.

2 Feb. That the Cape bulletin will be posted by eight o'clock.

He did not know. He left it as it stood. There were now two lines in the book with nothing under them, and the other had fifty-one days to run. For the first time in a hundred and nine quarters he could not have said which word would go there.

He turned back to the first page, where the one error stood. He did not correct it. He never had.

Below stairs, the steward went round the house as he had gone round it every night since 1872. He had paid once for a gas jet that burned for eighty days. He had turned out every jet in that house since, his own and other people's.

In the hall he reached up to the bracket, and stopped, and took his hand down.

He left it burning, and went to bed.
