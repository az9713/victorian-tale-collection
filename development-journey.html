These stories were written in partnership between a human writer and an AI drafting assistant. The assistant wrote the prose; the human writer chose the story, set its rules, and sent the drafts back until they read right. What follows is a short record of that steering, in the order it happened.

The Premisechosen before a word of prose was written

The human writer began with a pairing: Sherlock Holmes and Phileas Fogg, two exacting Londoners whose lives overlap for some thirty years, Fogg ageing from his fifties into his seventies while Holmes rises from obscurity into his prime. The backdrop was to be the whole late Victorian age: industry, Empire, unrest, and the old Queen's long ending.

The assistant drafted three possible story shapes. The human writer layered on requirements one at a time, then picked The Wager Ledger: five self-contained meetings between 1881 and 1909, with Fogg's ledger of private wagers as the thread, and a dry, witty, clockwork tone.

The Writer’s Rulesset by the human writer, kept in every draft

  1. Hints, never names. Nods to Around the World in Eighty Days and to Holmes’s cases must stay buried in objects, habits, and asides, with no titles, characters, or events named outright.
  2. Companions in the margins. Watson and Passepartout appear only briefly, as warm, talkative foils to two cold principals, saving larger roles for later stories.
  3. A short cast list of ghosts. Inspector Fix as a bitter, failed would-be detective; an ageing Aouda as Fogg’s one crack; one meeting between the Baker Street boys and Passepartout.
  4. Felt, not stated. Both men treat life as a solvable equation, and the cost of that should land in a late scene without anyone saying it.
  5. Quality over speed. Polished prose, one segment at a time, with a check-in after each.

Book the First: The Snuffboxthree drafts to an unforced meeting

The first draft had Holmes cross a card room to Fogg, the two trading deductions, and the ledger noting Holmes as a man “to be watched.” The human writer judged it staged, and asked the assistant to say honestly whether it agreed. It did.

The meeting should feel as accidental as Holmes and Watson’s in the hospital laboratory: two men colliding through pure circumstance, neither caring about the other.

The second draft made the encounter accidental. The human writer then reshaped it once more: Holmes should notice an unnamed gentleman and count his steps through the fog before the reader learns who he is, and the thing that catches Holmes’s eye should be that man’s composure when his whist partner is exposed as a cheat. The third draft did exactly that, keeping Fogg nameless until the ledger’s flyleaf in the closing line. The human writer approved it.

Book the Second: The Cablewritten to the human writer’s brief

For 1887 the human writer specified the case (a forged telegram at the Reform Club that nearly ruins a member), the Golden Jubilee as atmosphere rather than exposition, the gas-jet clue, and the boys trading London shortcuts with Passepartout. The assistant built the plot inside those walls: a cable interruption recorded in Fogg’s ledger proves the “Cape” telegram was written in London.

One problem surfaced in the drafting. Holmes was to be newly famous, but the first account of his work appeared in print only that November, months after the June Jubilee. The assistant made his fame come from Scotland Yard and the newspapers, with the doctor’s manuscript still forthcoming, and offered the human writer the alternative of moving the story to December.
