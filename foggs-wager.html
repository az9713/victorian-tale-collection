Fogg's Wager

April 1894
The Wager Ledger
An illustrated edition · Complete original story

At noon on Thursday, the fifth of April, 1894, the Candlewick Life Office would pay four thousand pounds on the death of Mr. Sherlock Holmes. Mr. Phileas Fogg had it in writing that Mr. Holmes was not dead.

He had had it in writing for nearly three years. The green ledger lay open on the desk at 7 Savile Row, at a page he seldom turned back to. Almost every line in that book named a minute. This one named a man.

7 May 1891. That Venn's guest is not dead. Term, three years. One sovereign.

Under it there was no second line. Mr. Fogg's wagers were settled by nightfall as a rule, and marked Won or Lost while the ink above was hardly dry. This one had stood open for one thousand and sixty-four days. It had thirty-two left to run.

He was sixty-two that spring, white-haired and upright, and he had been reading one letter since half past eight.

It had come by the last post on Wednesday, from the actuary of the Candlewick, a gentleman unknown to him. The wager had been a joke at the Reform Club for a week in the May of 1891, and a joke travels east along the Strand as readily as money does. The actuary wrote that a claim on the life of Mr. Holmes fell due on Thursday. The proof of death had come into the office on the fifth of January, and the policy allowed three calendar months from that day. The actuary had used every one of them. On Thursday the claims committee would sit at eleven and rise at noon, and when it rose the cheque would be drawn.

He understood Mr. Fogg to hold a contrary opinion. If Mr. Fogg held it for reasons, would he bring them to Cornhill by eleven?

Mr. Fogg held it for three reasons, and he had ordered a hansom for twenty minutes past ten. It was the last paragraph of the letter that kept him. The proof of death, the actuary wrote, was the sworn declaration of a private inquiry agent, who had seen the body.

There had been no body. That was the first of the three reasons. Somebody had sworn to one all the same, and at nine o'clock the street bell rang.

The steward went up to answer it. He was fifty-two, round and pink, and he had opened doors on four continents without being much surprised by what stood behind them. Behind this one stood a small, slight man of fifty-seven in a brown overcoat, whose eyebrows went up and down without his leave. The steward knew him before the hat was off. The last time this man had stood him a drink, he had woken up at sea.

"You," said the steward.

"Me," said the inquiry agent. "Tell him it's about noon today. He'll see me. He'll see anything with an hour in it."

Mr. Fogg received him standing, with his watch in his hand.

"I can give you nine minutes."

"One-and-twenty years," said the agent, looking round the study, "and you don't ask me to sit."

"You did not ask me, at Liverpool."

"I'll be short, then," said the agent. "The Candlewick has my declaration. I went to Meiringen. I saw what I saw, and I put my name to it before a commissioner. At eleven you mean to stand up in Cornhill with a betting book and tell three City men that I am a liar. Don't."

"I shall tell them three facts. They may draw the inference themselves."

"What facts?"

"First. On the seventh of May, 1891, the despatch in the English papers gave two men dead and no body recovered. Second. On the twenty-fourth of June, 1891, the rent of his rooms in Baker Street was paid. It has been paid every quarter since. That is twelve quarters, never a day late, and the rooms have not been let. My steward has friends in that street. Third. Last December the doctor printed his account. By that account he left his friend at the fall, was one hour going down and two coming back, and found a stick against a rock, a silver cigarette-case on the rock, and a letter under the case. He found no man. For three hours nobody was looking."

"A rent book and a sixpenny magazine!" The agent laughed, shortly. "Families pay for a dead man's rooms. It's sentiment."

"Sentiment pays for a quarter, or for four. It pays late, and it stops. Twelve times to the day is expectation."

"And you read the magazine."

"I read it. I gather that you did not."

"I don't read the doctor," said the agent. "I was taking thieves when his friend was in the schoolroom. Then the young man sets up as an amateur, and a doctor writes him up, and all at once it's a science. I followed one man round the world on a description and a likeness. I was wrong, and it finished me. He was never wrong, they tell me. Well, he's dead, and I have sworn to it, and it is the one thing I've got right." He put his hat on the desk, beside the ledger, and Mr. Fogg looked at the hat. "You knocked me down on the Saturday, and then you sent me money. Do you know what it is to be forgiven by cheque? I banked it. I've not forgiven you for it."

"It was a Friday," said Mr. Fogg.

"It was the twenty-first—"

"It was Friday, the twentieth. I was a day out. It is the only error in the ledger, and it stands on the first page."

The agent stared at him. He had come to Savile Row for several things, and that was not one of them. He took up his hat.

"I've a file on you, Mr. Fogg. It begins at Liverpool. I know the day and the hour of everything you've done since, and a thing or two before. Call me a perjurer in Cornhill this morning and it goes to a newspaper tonight."

"Is it accurate?"

"To the minute."

"Then I can have no objection to it. Your nine minutes are done."

At the door the agent turned.

"At noon one of us is finished. I've seen the body, Mr. Fogg. What have you seen?"

Mr. Fogg had seen nothing at all. He was aware of it, and at twenty minutes past ten he got into the hansom.

He wagered a sovereign with himself that the cab would set him down by nine minutes to eleven, and he lost it at the Mansion House, where an omnibus horse was down across the road. It left him two minutes for the actuary in place of nine.

The Candlewick had the first floor of an old house in a court off Cornhill. The ground floor had been a counting-house, and over its door two names could still be read under the paint. One of the two partners, the actuary said, had been dead seven years before the other thought of taking him down, and then had not done it.

The actuary was a spare, cheerful man of fifty-eight with a stiff leg and a stick. His father had been clerk in that counting-house at fifteen shillings a week, and he could show a visitor the grate that had once been allowed one coal.

"The law presumes a man dead in seven years," he said. "Short of that, an office may pay on proof that satisfies it. I have a declaration from a man who saw the body taken out of the river below the fall. The committee is satisfied. I am not, and I can't tell you why. The policy was taken out in March of '91, by a tall thin gentleman whom our doctor passed. In April it was assigned to a bill-discounter in Sherborne Lane. In May the newspapers drowned him. It is all in order. I distrust a death that is in order to the month."

"I was given out for dead myself, as a child, on very high authority," he added, in the passage. "An old gentleman who was a second father to me had seen my chair standing empty. He told me so years afterwards. He was mistaken, as it fell out; he took steps. I have wanted more than a prediction ever since, Mr. Fogg. I want more than a declaration now, and I have until noon to get it."

Through the glass of the outer office he pointed out a stout man who was reading one page of a newspaper for the third time. That was the assignee, come for his cheque. Mr. Fogg looked the other way, out of the passage window. In the court below, a man in a pea-jacket stood by the pump with his hands in his pockets. Mr. Fogg noticed him for one reason only. He stood with his back to the church clock.

"There's a bookseller, too," said the actuary, with his hand on the committee-room door. "From Kensington. He sent in his name at half past ten to say he has the doctor's magazine and something to say on the claim. I've let him in. I am at the stage of letting in booksellers."

It struck eleven. Mr. Fogg had three facts and a second-hand bookseller to set against a body.

Three men sat at the long table. The chairman was large and calm and had been chairman of something since he was thirty. The director on his left was deaf on whichever side a difficulty came from. The actuary sat on his right, and a clerk beyond him kept the minutes. At the foot of the table sat the inquiry agent with his hat on his knee. Mr. Fogg was given a chair by the window.

The bookseller had the chair nearest the door. He was an elderly, crooked man with white side-whiskers and a dozen books strapped under his arm, and before the sitting opened he had tried to sell the chairman a Catullus.

The clerk read the declaration. It stated that the deponent had gone to Meiringen for the assignee in August of 1893. A body had been taken from the river below the fall of Reichenbach while he was there. The deponent identified it as the body of Sherlock Holmes by the height, by what was left of the clothing, and by a silver cigarette-case found upon it.

The chairman asked Mr. Fogg for his reasons. Mr. Fogg gave them in forty seconds, with their dates.

"A missing body, a paid rent and a magazine," said the chairman, not unkindly. "And a wager."

"And a wager."

"Against which I have an oath."

"You have, gentlemen," said the agent, and he stood up to say it. "I stood on that bank. I'm an old police officer, and I have seen more drowned men than Mr. Fogg has seen wet days. Mr. Fogg was not there. He was in a club. His book can tell him when the Cape cable comes up. It cannot tell him who came out of that river."

"Asking your pardon, gentlemen," said a croaking voice by the door. "It's the cigarette-case."

The bookseller had a magazine open on his knee, a good deal thumbed.

"I sell this number, sirs. I've sold it forty times, and read it out to customers oftener than that. Here's the doctor at the fall. 'The silver cigarette-case which he used to carry.' It's on the rock, sirs, with the letter under it, and the doctor takes it up in his own hand. So it went home to Kensington in the doctor's pocket. It can't have been in a dead man's pocket in a river two years after."

"It is so printed," said Mr. Fogg.

The agent did not sit down.

"A man may own two cases," he said. "And I'll remind the committee what that is. It's a tale in a magazine, by the dead man's friend, written to sell. I gave you my oath. He gives you sixpennyworth."

The chairman looked at the clock, which said twenty-three minutes to twelve.

"You, sir, are a tradesman with a magazine. Mr. Fogg is a gentleman with a wager. I have a declaration with one doubtful article in it, and I have a claimant in my outer office. Only one thing answers a proof of death, and it is not an argument. Does any person in this room say, of his own knowledge, that this man is alive?"

"I say that he is not dead," said Mr. Fogg. "I do not say it of my own knowledge."

The bookseller looked at the clock. He looked at the window, and at the white-haired gentleman beside it, who had just declined by a hair's breadth to lie for the sake of his own wager. Something crossed the wizened face that had nothing of the bookseller in it.

"Then I must trouble you to send your clerk out, Mr. Chairman," he said, "and to lock the door after him."

The croak had gone out of the voice. The chairman, who had been about to refuse, found that he had nodded. The clerk went. The bookseller put his books on the floor and began to stand up, and he went on standing up for a foot longer than the committee expected.

Mr. Fogg had risen too. He did not hurry. He had hurried once in his life, and the man it had been spent on was sitting at the foot of the table. He took two paces to the window and drew down the blind, and it reached the sill at the moment it was wanted.

"Thank you," said the tall man. The white hair lay on the table with the whiskers. He was forty and very thin, and his face was a dead white. "Who is it?"

"A man in the court, by the pump. He has stood forty minutes with his back to the church clock. He is waiting, and he never asks the time. So it is a person he waits for."

"He is. I'm obliged to you. Gentlemen, my name is Sherlock Holmes. I should be glad if you would not pay four thousand pounds on my death. I have a use for the man who is to get it."

"You are the third since Christmas," said the chairman. "The doctor's account brought them out. One wrote from Leeds, and one called in person and was fetched away by his sister. You came into this room a bookseller and you stand in it a dead man. Who in London knows you?"

"Three persons. One is a doctor in Kensington, who thinks me dead and must think so until tonight. One is a landlady, who was in hysterics at nine this morning. One is my brother, who cannot be fetched from Pall Mall by noon and would not come. There is a policeman, but I want him tonight; send to Scotland Yard at noon, and all London has it by four. And there is a man who has met me twice. He has just drawn your blind."

"Mr. Fogg," said the chairman. "Is this Mr. Holmes?"

Mr. Fogg looked at the tall man for some seconds.

"I cannot say."

The agent laughed aloud.

"I have been in a room with Mr. Holmes twice," said Mr. Fogg. "In 1881 I saw him across a card room. In 1887 I spoke with him for some four minutes. That is six years and nine months ago. This gentleman is of the height. I cannot say more."

Holmes had crossed France to take a murderer, and he was to be stopped in Cornhill by an honest man. He found, somewhat to his surprise, that he did not resent it.

"Quite right," he said. "Then don't say it, Mr. Fogg. Look it up. You have the book in your pocket. The eleventh of October, 1881; a Tuesday. The card room of your club. I was there as Venn's guest, which I think is your phrase and not mine. At twenty-six minutes past nine I asked a captain for his snuffbox. The room got up. You did not. You said, 'I believe it is still my lead,' and you led the king of hearts. You played nine tricks to the end, against two men who could hardly hold their cards. You paid two gentlemen out of your pocketbook and never counted it. The rubber was given up at nineteen minutes to ten."

Mr. Fogg had the page in four seconds. He read it out.

11 Oct. That the second rubber will be concluded by a quarter to ten.
Lost. Rubber abandoned, 9h 41m. Cause: Venn's guest.

"Well?" said the chairman.

"The minute is right. Nine tricks is right. The notes were not counted." He paused. "The king of hearts is not in the ledger."

"No," said Holmes. "I rather thought it would be in you."

Mr. Fogg was silent for about as long as another man takes to smile.

"It was the king of hearts."

"Thirty men were in that room!" The agent was on his feet. "Any one of them could have sold him that. I could buy it myself in an afternoon."

"So you could. You have a gift for buying particulars," said Holmes. "Then here is one that was never said aloud. In June of '87 your steward carried a copy of one entry to my door, Mr. Fogg. The reading room heard what it lost you. Nobody heard what the wager was. You had wagered that the Cape bulletin would be posted by ten minutes past three."

The pages went forward to 1887.

14 June. That the Cape bulletin will be posted by 3h 10m.

Mr. Fogg closed the book.

"Gentlemen, this is Mr. Holmes. I say it of my own knowledge."

It was eleven minutes to noon. Everybody looked at the agent. He had gone the colour of the blind.

"You went to Meiringen," said Holmes, almost gently. "You are too old a hand to swear to a place you never saw. You saw the fall, and you saw no body, and you supplied one. All the world knew I was dead. It seemed a small thing to add."

"Two hundred guineas," said the agent. "I buried you for two hundred guineas, and I was glad of the work." His eyebrows had stopped. "Twice. Once round the world after him, and now you. The two of you, in one room."

Mr. Fogg consulted his watch.

"It wants eleven minutes of noon. At noon this committee acts on your declaration. A paper taken back before it is acted on may yet be called a mistake. You have eleven minutes in which to have been mistaken."

The agent stared. "You'd give me that."

"In 1872 you wanted only one thing of me, and it was delay. I have kept it by me. You may have eleven minutes of it."

Nobody in the room understood him but the agent, who understood him perfectly. He took the declaration from the chairman's hand, wrote four words across the face of it, and signed them.

"Then the proof is not satisfactory," said the chairman, "and the claim stands over. The minute will say that the deponent withdrew, and nothing else. I shall call this paper a mistake, sir, for as long as you hold your tongue. It stays in my safe. You have my word until tomorrow, Mr. Holmes."

"And a word for your own skin," said Holmes to the agent. "The man behind your bill-discounter shot a man on Friday. He will not thank you for this morning. Go by the back stairs, and go home."

"I know how to keep clear of a man I have crossed," said the agent. "I have had the practice."

He stopped at the door, beside Mr. Fogg.

"Don't send me money this time."

"No."

"I still have the file."

"I should hope so. I believe it is the only one."

Across the court the church clock began to strike twelve. In the outer office the stout man folded his newspaper, and no cheque was drawn. But the tall man had said that he had a use for him, and Mr. Fogg could not think what use a man newly alive might have for the receiver of four thousand pounds.

He was told in the actuary's room, while Holmes put the white hair on again at the actuary's shaving-glass.

"The stout man is nobody; a bill-discounter. The man behind him I have come a long way for. On Friday night he shot a young man in Park Lane through an open window, and I cannot prove it until he does it again. Tonight he will try it on me, at a window of my choosing. He lives by cards, and I believe the young man had found out how he wins. He has no money to speak of. With four thousand pounds in his pocket he might have liked Paris better than he likes me. I prefer him poor, and in London."

"He cheats at whist," said Mr. Fogg.

"He does."

"That is how you came to cost me a wager."

"It is how we met, Mr. Fogg. I seem to come back to it." The whiskers went on. "I must ask one thing. Not a word of this to any person until the morning papers. And not a line."

"The ledger is not read."

"Humour a dead man."

"Until the morning papers," said Mr. Fogg. "You were dead one thousand and sixty-seven days."

"The doctor makes it three years."

"The doctor rounds."

Holmes laughed, which the bookseller ought not to have done, and stooped for his books.

"I'll tell you something in exchange. In '81 I walked behind you in the fog from Waterloo Place, and I counted your paces between the lamps. They never varied. I have thought since that you had a right to know you were counted."

"I count them myself."

"And now a thing you won't thank me for. Your wager is dated the seventh of May. I heard your three reasons. The first is not a fact; it is the want of one. The second is dated seven weeks after the wager. The third is dated two years and a half after it. You had not one of them when you wrote that line."

"No."

"You staked a sovereign on a man, with nothing in your hand. You, of all men in London. Why?"

Mr. Fogg put on his hat.

"A hand that has been dealt is played out. Yours was left on the table. I do not care for an abandoned rubber."

"That explains the wish. It does not explain the sovereign."

"No," Mr. Fogg agreed.

Holmes had lost a foot of his height again. He looked up from under the white eyebrows with something very near affection.

"Nobody has yet paid me to find out why. I begin to think I shall have to do it for nothing. Good day, Mr. Fogg. I am expected in an armchair at two."

He went down the back stairs, crooked and slow, with a dozen books under his arm. The man by the pump did not turn his head. Mr. Fogg watched from the passage window until the court was empty. It came to him then that he had found his man alive at eleven minutes to noon, and had let him walk away to be shot at.

He reached the Reform Club fifty-five minutes late, which had never happened. He played his whist, and in the second rubber he had to be told that it was his lead.

At Savile Row that evening the ledger lay open at the seventh of May, 1891. He had won. There was a pen in the tray, and a promise between him and the page, and he sat before both for a long while. He found that a man could win a wager at noon and still want its subject alive at midnight. He could find no line in the book for that.

At ten o'clock he went out. He told nobody. He ordered no cab, and for the first time since he could remember he walked a mile and did not count it.

It was a bleak and boisterous night, and the wind came down Baker Street with nothing to stop it.

Mr. Fogg stood under a lamp at the corner of Blandford Street. Across the way and a little to the north, one window was lit behind a yellow blind, and on the blind lay the black shadow of a man seated in a chair. Mr. Fogg knew whose rooms they were.

At a quarter past eleven the shadow turned its head. At the half-hour it turned again, forty seconds late. At a quarter to twelve it turned a third time, and it was forty seconds late again.

No living man moves by the clock. Mr. Fogg knew of one exception, and he was standing in the street. The thing in the window was a figure, and somebody was shifting it by a kitchen clock that ran forty seconds slow. So that was the window of his choosing. The bait was out. The man who had set it was somewhere near, in the dark, and so was the other.

There were things in that street which Mr. Fogg did not see. Two men stood in a doorway farther up, out of a wind that did not blow into it, and they had been looking at him for some time. He had stood under that lamp for the better part of an hour, with a watch in his hand and his eyes on a lighted window. To a policeman, that is a murderer waiting. Standing still had got Mr. Fogg arrested only once before, and that was at Liverpool.

One of the two had begun to cross the road when the steward came round the corner at a trot, with a greatcoat over his arm.

"Monsieur! Without the coat! At midnight, in this wind, at your age!" He said it at the top of his voice, in the English of a man who has been frightened for an hour. "I go to the study and the chair is empty. Never in my service does he go out at ten o'clock, and tonight he goes!"

Mr. Fogg permitted himself to be buttoned into the coat. The man in the road stopped. He considered an old gentleman who had wandered and a servant who had found him, and he went back to his doorway.

They turned east along Blandford Street, the steward still scolding. A man was coming towards them on the other pavement. He was elderly and lean, with a huge grizzled moustache, an opera-hat on the back of his head, and a shirt-front that gleamed where his overcoat hung open. He carried a stick without leaning on it, a little tenderly, like something with works in it.

He stopped at the mouth of a narrow passage and looked across the road. For as long as it takes to judge a distance, one of the best shots that India ever sent home considered whether a white-haired gentleman and a scolding Frenchman had anything to do with him. Then he turned down the passage and was gone.

"That man has served somewhere hot," said Mr. Fogg. He had seen that brown, creased look once before, across a card table. "He cheats at whist."

"Monsieur?"

"Walk on."

They had gone two hundred yards when it came from behind them: a long, thin tinkle of broken glass. After it came a whistle, and then boots, several pairs, running towards the sound and not away from it.

Mr. Fogg stopped. He looked at his watch. It was four minutes past midnight. He stood and listened for a cry, for a voice, for anything with a meaning in it, and the wind brought him nothing more.

"What is it, monsieur? What has happened?"

"I do not know," said Mr. Fogg.

In one-and-twenty years the steward had never heard him say it.

At seven the next morning the steward came up to the hall and found a gas jet burning in the daylight.

It was the jet in the study. Under it his master sat asleep in the chair, in yesterday's clothes, with the green ledger open before him and a dry pen in the tray. The steward had never seen such a thing, and he stood in the doorway for some time.

He had the morning paper in his hand. An officer of the Indian Army had been taken in Baker Street in the night for the Park Lane murder. An inspector of Scotland Yard had the whole credit of it. The paper had no other name to give. The steward had read the column twice and understood about half of it, which was more than he usually asked of an English newspaper. He laid it open on the desk, where it would be seen first.

He did not read the ledger. He never did. Had he looked, he would have seen a line three years old with nothing written under it, and beside it a man who had sat up all night for leave to write one word. He did not close the book that morning, because his master's hand lay on it.

Then he reached up to the bracket. Somebody always pays for a jet left burning. He had known it since 1872, and he knew the figure by heart. He turned out the gas, and for once he could not think of anybody to send the bill to.
